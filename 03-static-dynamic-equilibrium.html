Static vs Dynamic Equilibrium

Deconstructing reaction rates, microscopic kinetic activity, macroscopic constancy, collision mechanics, and graphical representations under NESA Stage 6 Chemistry standards.

NESA Syllabus Inquiry Focus

Stage 6 Chemistry

Inquiry Question: What happens when chemical reactions do not go through completion?

Syllabus Outcomes Addressed:

  • Account for the characteristics of a dynamic equilibrium system using collision theory (CH-12-01).
  • Explain why macroscopic properties remain constant at equilibrium.
  • Compare static and dynamic equilibrium systems using chemical and physical exemplars.
  • Analyse graphical representations of reaction rate vs time and concentration vs time for equilibrium systems.

1. Defining Chemical Equilibrium States

Thermodynamic Mechanics

In HSC Chemistry, understanding equilibrium requires categorising systems based on whether molecular activity continues once observable macroscopic changes cease. A system is said to be in equilibrium when its macroscopic properties—such as concentration, colour intensity, gas pressure, temperature, and total mass—remain completely constant over time.

However, the underlying physical and chemical processes occurring at the sub-microscopic level can differ fundamentally. We classify equilibrium into two primary forms: Dynamic Equilibrium and Static Equilibrium.

🔄

Dynamic Equilibrium

A state achieved in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction ($r_{\mathrm{f}}$) equals the rate of the reverse reaction ($r_{\mathrm{r}}$), and both rates are strictly non-zero ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} > 0$).

  • Microscopic Activity: Continuous and ongoing in both directions simultaneously. Reactants transform into products while products recombine into reactants at identical speeds.
  • Macroscopic Properties: Strictly constant over time because the net rates of change for all species are zero.
  • Requirement: Must take place in a closed system where neither matter nor energy escapes unhindered, and the reaction must be reversible.
⏸️

Static Equilibrium

A state achieved when the rate of the forward reaction and the rate of the reverse reaction both equal zero ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} = 0$).

  • Microscopic Activity: Complete cessation of chemical movement or interconversion between chemical species. No forward or reverse collisions occur.
  • Macroscopic Properties: Strictly constant over time, but purely because no microscopic reaction is occurring in either direction.
  • Context: Common in irreversible chemical processes that have gone to $100\%$ completion, or physical systems with insurmountable activation energy barriers.

Essential Distinction: Dynamic Equilibrium vs Steady State

Do not confuse Dynamic Equilibrium with a Steady State. In a steady state (such as a flowing river, a continuous industrial reactor, or metabolic pathways in living organisms), system properties remain constant over time because matter and energy enter and leave the system at equal rates. However, a steady state occurs in an open system and requires a continuous flux of energy and matter. Dynamic equilibrium occurs strictly in a closed system where energy exchange may occur, but mass exchange cannot ($\Delta m = 0$).

2. Microscopic Kinetics & Collision Theory Progression

Molecular Dynamics

To understand how dynamic equilibrium is established from a mixture of pure reactants, we analyse the system chronologically using Collision Theory. Consider a generic reversible gas-phase reaction inside a sealed container at constant temperature:

$$\mathrm{A(g) + B(g) \rightleftharpoons C(g) + D(g)}$$

1. Initial State ($t = 0$)

At the start of the reaction, only reactants $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ are present in high concentrations.

  • Collision frequency between $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ is at its maximum value.
  • Forward reaction rate is at its highest point ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = \text{Maximum}$).
  • Because no product molecules ($\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$) exist yet, $[\mathrm{C}] = 0$ and $[\mathrm{D}] = 0$.
  • The reverse reaction rate is strictly zero ($r_{\mathrm{r}} = 0$).

2. Intermediate State ($0 < t < t_{\mathrm{eq}}$)

As reactant molecules collide successfully, they transform into products over time.

  • Concentrations of $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ decrease ($[\mathrm{A}], [\mathrm{B}] \downarrow$). Forward collision frequency drops, so $r_{\mathrm{f}}$ decreases continuously.
  • Concentrations of $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ accumulate ($[\mathrm{C}], [\mathrm{D}] \uparrow$).
  • Collisions between product molecules start occurring, initiating the reverse reaction. Thus, $r_{\mathrm{r}}$ increases continuously from zero.

3. Dynamic Equilibrium ($t \ge t_{\mathrm{eq}}$)

Eventually, the decreasing forward rate matches the rising reverse rate.

  • The rate of product formation exactly matches the rate of product consumption ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$).
  • Every second, the exact number of reactant molecules converted to products is replenished by product molecules reforming reactants.
  • Macroscopic properties freeze into a constant steady appearance, despite intense microscopic molecular activity.

Common Misconception Warning (Crucial NESA Exam Focus!)

Misconception: "At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products must be equal ($[\text{Reactants}] = [\text{Products}]$)."
Fact: At dynamic equilibrium, the RATES of forward and reverse reactions are equal ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$). The CONCENTRATIONS of reactants and products are CONSTANT, but they are almost never equal to each other!
Depending on the activation energy and thermodynamic stability of species, a system at equilibrium may consist of $99\%$ products and $1\%$ reactants, or vice versa, while maintaining $r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$.

4. Real-World Exemplars of Chemical & Physical Systems

HSC Syllabus Depth

NESA syllabus directives require students to analyse specific chemical and physical examples for both dynamic and static equilibrium states.

Category A: Dynamic Equilibrium Exemplars

1. Liquid–Vapour Phase Equilibrium

System: Water in a stoppered conical flask at constant temperature.

$$\mathrm{H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons H_2O(g)}$$

In a closed container, liquid water vaporises into the air space above it. As gaseous water vapour accumulates, the rate of condensation ($r_{\mathrm{cond}}$) increases until it equals the rate of evaporation ($r_{\mathrm{evap}}$). Liquid level and vapour pressure remain constant, but water molecules continuously cycle between phase states.

2. Saturated Solution Equilibrium

System: Undissolved solid sodium chloride in contact with a saturated solution.

$$\mathrm{NaCl(s) \rightleftharpoons Na^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)}$$

Solid $\mathrm{NaCl}$ dissolves into aqueous ions at rate $r_{\mathrm{dissolution}}$. Simultaneously, dissolved $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ ions collide with the solid crystal lattice and precipitate out at rate $r_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$. When $r_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = r_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$, mass of undissolved solid and ion concentration remain constant.

3. Dinitrogen Tetroxide Gas Equilibrium

System: Colourless $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$ gas decomposing into brown $\mathrm{NO_2}$ gas in a sealed syringe.

$$\mathrm{\underset{(Colourless)}{N_2O_4(g)} \rightleftharpoons \underset{(Brown)}{2NO_2(g)}}$$

$\mathrm{N_2O_4}$ breaks down to form brown $\mathrm{NO_2}$. As $[\mathrm{NO_2}]$ builds up, two $\mathrm{NO_2}$ molecules collide to reform $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$. At equilibrium, the intensity of the brown colour remains perfectly constant, showing macroscopically frozen visual properties while microscopic interconversion continues at equal rates.

Category B: Static Equilibrium Exemplars

1. Irreversible Complete Reactions

System: Combustion of magnesium ribbon in oxygen.

$$\mathrm{2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2MgO(s)}$$

Once all magnesium ribbon is consumed, the reaction stops entirely. The forward reaction rate drops to zero ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = 0$). Magnesium oxide does not spontaneously decompose back into magnesium and oxygen gas ($r_{\mathrm{r}} = 0$). The system reaches static equilibrium where macroscopically nothing changes because microscopic chemical activity has completely ceased.

2. Solid Carbon Allotrope Conversion

System: Diamond transforming into Graphite at standard ambient temperature and pressure.

$$\mathrm{C(diamond) \rightarrow C(graphite)}$$

Although graphite is thermodynamically more stable than diamond ($\Delta G^\circ < 0$), the activation energy barrier for breaking covalent network tetrahedral bonds is extremely high. At room temperature, the rate of transformation is practically zero ($r_{\mathrm{f}} \approx 0, r_{\mathrm{r}} \approx 0$). The system remains locked in a static equilibrium state.

6. Worked Examples (SCAFFOLDED NESA EXAM SOLUTIONS)

HSC Step-by-Step Solutions

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Classifying Physical & Chemical Equilibrium

2 Marks

Classify each of the following scenarios as either a dynamic equilibrium, a static equilibrium, or a non-equilibrium state:
a) A sealed bottle of sparkling water kept in a refrigerator at constant temperature.
b) Complete combustion of propane gas ($\mathrm{C_3H_8}$) in excess oxygen in an open barbecue grill.

Model Solution:

a) Dynamic Equilibrium: The sealed bottle is a closed system containing dissolved carbon dioxide gas and gaseous carbon dioxide in the headspace ($\mathrm{CO_{2}(aq) \rightleftharpoons CO_{2}(g)}$). Dissolution and gas release occur at equal, non-zero rates ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} > 0$).

b) Non-Equilibrium State (Irreversible Completion): Combustion of propane is an irreversible reaction ($\mathrm{C_3H_8 + 5O_2 \rightarrow 3CO_2 + 4H_2O}$) in an open system where products escape into the air. Once fuel is depleted, rates drop to zero ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} = 0$).

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Interpreting Equilibrium Rate Graphs

3 Marks

A student recorded the forward and reverse rates of a gas reaction $\mathrm{2X(g) \rightleftharpoons Y(g)}$ inside a $1.0\text{ L}$ rigid sealed container over $10\text{ minutes}$.
• At $t = 0\text{ min}$, $r_{\mathrm{forward}} = 0.80\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ and $r_{\mathrm{reverse}} = 0$.
• At $t = 4\text{ min}$, $r_{\mathrm{forward}} = 0.35\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ and $r_{\mathrm{reverse}} = 0.35\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$.
• At $t = 8\text{ min}$, $r_{\mathrm{forward}} = 0.35\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ and $r_{\mathrm{reverse}} = 0.35\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$.
Explain what state the system reaches at $t = 4\text{ min}$ and justify why concentration remains constant between $t = 4\text{ min}$ and $t = 8\text{ min}$.

Model Solution:

1. System State Identification: At $t = 4\text{ min}$, the system reaches dynamic equilibrium because $r_{\mathrm{forward}} = r_{\mathrm{reverse}} = 0.35\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1} > 0$.

2. Justification using Kinetics: Between $t = 4\text{ min}$ and $t = 8\text{ min}$, species $\mathrm{X}$ is being consumed by the forward reaction at the exact same rate ($0.35\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$) that it is being produced by the reverse reaction. Because the rate of formation equals the rate of consumption, the net rate of change for all concentrations is zero ($\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{X}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0$). Thus, concentrations remain strictly constant over this time interval.

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Radioactive Isotope Tracer Proof of Dynamic Equilibrium

4 Marks

A chemist prepares a saturated solution of sodium chloride ($\mathrm{NaCl}$) in contact with undissolved solid $\mathrm{NaCl(s)}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$.
She then adds a tiny quantity of solid radioactive sodium chloride containing the isotope Chlorine-36 ($\mathrm{Na^{36}Cl(s)}$) to the flask without altering the temperature or liquid volume.
Predict and explain what will happen to the radioactivity level in the liquid solution over time, and explain how this experiment proves that the saturated solution is in dynamic equilibrium rather than static equilibrium.

Model Solution:

1. Prediction: The radioactivity level in the aqueous solution will initially be zero, but will continuously increase over time until it reaches a constant, stable maximum reading.

2. Microscopic Dynamic Explanation: In a saturated solution, dynamic dissolution equilibrium exists between undissolved solid crystal lattice and dissolved ions: $$\mathrm{NaCl(s) \rightleftharpoons Na^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)}$$ Because the system is in dynamic equilibrium ($r_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = r_{\mathrm{crystallisation}} > 0$), solid radioactive $\mathrm{Na^{36}Cl(s)}$ dissolves into the liquid phase at rate $r_{\mathrm{dissolution}}$. Simultaneously, non-radioactive ions in solution crystallise back onto the solid lattice at rate $r_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$.

3. Proof of Dynamic vs Static State: If the equilibrium were static ($r_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = r_{\mathrm{crystallisation}} = 0$), no radioactive $\mathrm{^{36}Cl^-}$ ions could enter the liquid phase, and the solution would remain non-radioactive indefinitely. The continuous appearance and redistribution of radioactivity in the aqueous phase proves conclusively that dissolution and crystallisation are ongoing microscopic processes occurring at equal rates.

Worked Example 4 (HSC Exam Challenge): Stoichiometry, Vessel Boundaries, and Static vs Dynamic Equilibrium

5 Marks

A chemistry student investigates the thermal decomposition of solid calcium carbonate in two separate $2.00\text{ L}$ vessels at $800^\circ\text{C}$: $$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \rightleftharpoons CaO(s) + CO_2(g)}$$ • Vessel 1 is an unsealed, open vessel containing an initial $10.0\text{ g}$ sample of $\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)}$.
• Vessel 2 is a sealed, rigid $2.00\text{ L}$ vessel containing an initial $10.0\text{ g}$ sample of $\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)}$.

After heating both vessels for 60 minutes:
• In Vessel 1, the mass of remaining solid is $5.60\text{ g}$ and remains constant thereafter.
• In Vessel 2, the mass of remaining solid is $7.50\text{ g}$ and remains constant thereafter.

a) Calculate the mass of $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ produced in Vessel 1 at the end of 60 minutes, and identify the equilibrium state (static, dynamic, or non-equilibrium) reached in Vessel 1. Justify your answer. (2 Marks)
b) Account for why Vessel 2 contains a larger mass of remaining solid ($7.50\text{ g}$) than Vessel 1 ($5.60\text{ g}$), and explain using collision theory how Vessel 2 established dynamic equilibrium rather than static equilibrium. (3 Marks)

Model Solution:

a) Mass Calculation & Vessel 1 State:

Molar mass of $\mathrm{CaCO_3} = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
Molar mass of $\mathrm{CaO} = 40.08 + 16.00 = 56.08\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
Initial moles of $\mathrm{CaCO_3} = \frac{10.0\text{ g}}{100.09\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.09991\text{ mol}$.
If all $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ decomposes completely: mass of $\mathrm{CaO}$ formed = $0.09991\text{ mol} \times 56.08\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 5.603\text{ g} \approx 5.60\text{ g}$.
Because the remaining solid in Vessel 1 is exactly $5.60\text{ g}$, $100\%$ of the $10.0\text{ g}$ sample decomposed into $\mathrm{CaO(s)}$ solid.
Mass of $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ evolved and lost = $10.0\text{ g} - 5.60\text{ g} = 4.40\text{ g}$.
Equilibrium State: Vessel 1 has reached a non-equilibrium complete completion state (or static state). Because the open system allowed $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ to escape freely, no product gas remained to undergo reverse collisions. Once all $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ was consumed, $r_{\mathrm{forward}} = r_{\mathrm{reverse}} = 0$.

b) Analysis of Vessel 2 (Closed System Dynamic Equilibrium):

In Vessel 2, the sealed container prevented $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ from escaping ($\Delta m = 0$). As $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ gas accumulated inside the sealed space, collision frequency between gaseous $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and solid $\mathrm{CaO}$ increased, initiating the reverse reaction.
Equilibrium was reached when the rate of reverse recombination matched the rate of forward decomposition ($r_{\mathrm{forward}} = r_{\mathrm{reverse}} > 0$). At this point, net decomposition stopped, leaving $7.50\text{ g}$ of solid mixture ($5.69\text{ g}$ decomposed $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ and $4.31\text{ g}$ unreacted $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$). In contrast, Vessel 1 proceeded to $100\%$ completion ($5.60\text{ g}$ solid remaining) because continuous product escape prevented reverse collisions from occurring.

Question 1 (Easy): Distinguishing Equilibrium Types

2 Marks

Distinguish between static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium in terms of microscopic processes and forward/reverse reaction rates.

Hint: Focus on whether microscopic chemical interconversion is still occurring, and express the relationship between $r_{\mathrm{f}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{r}}$ using mathematical symbols ($> 0$ vs $= 0$).

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer: In a dynamic equilibrium, microscopic forward and reverse processes continue constantly at equal, non-zero rates ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} > 0$). In contrast, in a static equilibrium, all microscopic chemical interconversions have completely ceased, and both forward and reverse reaction rates drop to zero ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} = 0$). Both states exhibit constant macroscopic properties.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 2 Marks: Correctly contrasts microscopic activity AND rate equations ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} > 0$ vs $r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}} = 0$) for both systems.
  • 1 Mark: Provides a partial description without explicitly contrasting rates or microscopic details.

Question 2 (Medium): Macroscopic Constancy via Collision Theory

3 Marks

Explain, using collision theory, why the colour intensity of a sealed flask containing $\mathrm{N_2O_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO_2(g)}$ remains completely unchanged once dynamic equilibrium is established.

Hint: Connect the colour intensity directly to the concentration of brown $\mathrm{NO_2(g)}$ molecules and state why $[\mathrm{NO_2}]$ stops changing in terms of collision frequency.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer: Colour intensity depends directly on the concentration of brown $\mathrm{NO_2(g)}$ molecules inside the vessel. At dynamic equilibrium, the forward collision rate of colourless $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$ molecules breaking apart equals the reverse collision rate of brown $\mathrm{NO_2}$ molecules recombining ($r_{\mathrm{forward}} = r_{\mathrm{reverse}}$). Because $\mathrm{NO_2}$ is produced and consumed at identical rates, its net concentration remains perfectly constant ($\Delta [\mathrm{NO_2}] = 0$). Consequently, the macroscopic colour intensity remains completely unchanged over time.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 3 Marks: Identifies $\mathrm{NO_2}$ as the coloured species, links constant colour to equal forward and reverse collision rates, and concludes net rate of concentration change is zero.
  • 2 Marks: Connects equilibrium to equal rates, but omits explicit collision theory phrasing or concentration details.
  • 1 Mark: States that colour is constant because the system is at equilibrium.

Question 3 (Hard): Graph Analysis & Misconception Evaluation

4 Marks

A student draws a concentration vs time graph for a reaction $\mathrm{A(g) \rightleftharpoons B(g)}$ where the curve for reactant $\mathrm{A}$ decreases and crosses the curve for product $\mathrm{B}$ at $t = 3\text{ minutes}$. The student claims: "The reaction reached dynamic equilibrium at $t = 3\text{ minutes}$ because the concentration lines intersect."
Evaluate the student's statement, correct any errors, and describe what feature on a concentration graph actually indicates the achievement of equilibrium.

Hint: What does an intersection point on a concentration graph signify? Is $[\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{B}]$ required for equilibrium?

Model Answer & Rubric

Evaluation: The student's claim is incorrect.

Correction: The intersection point at $t = 3\text{ minutes}$ merely indicates the moment when $[\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{B}]$. Dynamic equilibrium does NOT require reactant and product concentrations to be equal; it only requires forward and reverse reaction rates to be equal ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$).

Identifying Feature on Graph: On a concentration vs time graph, dynamic equilibrium is indicated when concentration curves for all species become completely horizontal (plateau), showing that concentrations have become constant over time ($\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0$). This horizontal plateau occurs after $t = 3\text{ minutes}$ when rates equilibrate.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 4 Marks: Correctly evaluates statement as false; explains that curve intersection means $[\mathrm{A}]=[\mathrm{B}]$ not $r_f=r_r$; clarifies that concentrations need only be constant; explicitly identifies horizontal plateaus as the true graphical marker.
  • 2-3 Marks: Identifies that concentrations do not need to be equal, but gives an incomplete explanation of graphical plateaus.
  • 1 Mark: States that the student is wrong without clear justification.

1. Molecular Structure & Intermolecular Interaction Forces

Foundational Mechanisms

To comprehend why ionic solid crystals dissolve in water, we must examine the sub-microscopic structural properties of both the solute (the ionic crystal lattice) and the solvent (liquid water).

1. The Structure and Polarity of Water ($\mathrm{H_2O}$)

Liquid water consists of polar covalent molecules with a non-linear (bent) molecular geometry and a bond angle of approximately $104.5^\circ$.

  • Electronegativity Difference: Oxygen ($\text{EN} = 3.44$) is significantly more electronegative than hydrogen ($\text{EN} = 2.20$). As a result, electron density in each $\mathrm{O-H}$ covalent bond is drawn strongly towards the oxygen atom.
  • Permanent Dipole Moment: Due to its asymmetrical bent geometry, the two bond dipoles reinforce one another rather than cancelling out. This gives water a permanent molecular dipole with a partial negative charge ($\delta^-$) centered on the oxygen atom and partial positive charges ($\delta^+$) located on the two hydrogen atoms.

2. The Ionic Crystal Lattice

An ionic solid (such as sodium chloride, $\mathrm{NaCl}$) exists as a rigid, highly ordered three-dimensional lattice composed of alternating cations ($\mathrm{Na^+}$) and anions ($\mathrm{Cl^-}$).

The ions are held together in fixed lattice positions by strong, non-directional electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions (ionic bonding). The energy required to break one mole of an ionic crystal lattice into its separated gaseous ions is termed the lattice dissociation enthalpy ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}}$):

$$\mathrm{NaCl(s) \to Na^+(g) + Cl^-(g)} \quad \Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}} = +787\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$

2. The Step-by-Step Molecular Dissolution Mechanism

Sub-microscopic Step Analysis

Dissolution is not a instantaneous single event; it is a progressive molecular process occurring at the solid-liquid interface. When an ionic solid is placed into water, dissolution occurs through three consecutive steps:

Step 1: Water Molecular Orientation

Water molecules constantly undergoing random thermal motion collide with the surface of the ionic crystal. Polar water molecules reorient themselves according to electrostatic charge attraction:

  • The partially negative oxygen atoms ($\delta^-$) align towards exposed surface cations ($\mathrm{Na^+}$).
  • The partially positive hydrogen atoms ($\delta^+$) align towards exposed surface anions ($\mathrm{Cl^-}$).

Step 2: Ion Dislodgement & Lattice Breaking

As multiple water molecules cluster around a surface ion, the sum of the newly formed ion-dipole attractive forces exerts a pulling force on that ion.

When the collective ion-dipole attractive energy overcomes the electrostatic ionic bonds holding the ion in the crystal lattice, the ion is dislodged from the surface and pulled into the liquid solvent phase.

Step 3: Solvation / Hydration Shell Formation

Once pulled into solution, the free ion is completely surrounded by a stable arrangement of water molecules, termed a hydration shell (or solvation shell).

This exergonic process is called hydration. The formation of ion-dipole bonds releases energy into the system, stabilising the mobile hydrated ions ($\mathrm{Na^+(aq)}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)}$) and preventing them from immediately recombining into the crystal lattice.

Chemical Equation for Ionic Dissolution

The overall dissolution process is written showing the solid ionic compound producing separated aqueous ions: $$\mathrm{NaCl(s) \xrightarrow{H_2O(l)} Na^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)}$$ Note that $\mathrm{H_2O(l)}$ is written above the reaction arrow to denote its role as the solvent medium rather than a stoichiometric reactant.

3. Thermochemistry: Enthalpy of Solution ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$)

Hess's Law Energetics

The overall energy change accompanying dissolution is known as the molar enthalpy of solution ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$). It is defined as the enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solute dissolves completely in an excess of solvent to form an infinitely dilute solution at standard pressure ($100\text{ kPa}$).

Using Hess's Law, the dissolution process can be broken down into a two-step theoretical thermodynamic cycle:

The Thermochemical Dissolution Cycle

1. Lattice Separation ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}}$): Breaking the solid ionic lattice into gaseous ions. This step requires energy input to overcome ionic bonds and is strictly ENDOTHERMIC ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}} > 0$): $$\mathrm{MX(s) \to M^+(g) + X^-(g)} \quad \Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}} > 0$$

2. Gaseous Ion Hydration ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}}$): Dissolving gaseous ions into water to form hydrated aqueous ions. This step forms strong ion-dipole attractions and is strictly EXOTHERMIC ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}} < 0$): $$\mathrm{M^+(g) + X^-(g) \xrightarrow{H_2O(l)} M^+(aq) + X^-(aq)} \quad \Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}} < 0$$

3. Hess's Law Summation: $$\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}}$$

Exothermic vs Endothermic Dissolution

Depending on the relative magnitudes of lattice enthalpy ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}}$) and hydration enthalpy ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}}$), the overall enthalpy of solution ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$) can be negative or positive:

Exothermic Dissolution ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} < 0$)

Occurs when the energy released during ion hydration is greater than the energy required to break the crystal lattice: $$|\Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}}| > |\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}}|$$ Thermal energy is released to the surroundings, causing the solution temperature to increase (e.g. dissolution of $\mathrm{NaOH}$ or $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$).

Endothermic Dissolution ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$)

Occurs when the energy required to break the crystal lattice exceeds the energy released during ion hydration: $$|\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}}| > |\Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}}|$$ Thermal energy is absorbed from the surroundings, causing the solution temperature to decrease (e.g. dissolution of $\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}$ or $\mathrm{KNO_3}$).

4. Entropy ($\Delta S$) & Spontaneity ($\Delta G$) of Dissolution

Thermodynamic Spontaneity

A major inquiry question in Module 5 chemistry is: Why do some ionic compounds dissolve endothermically ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$) even though endothermic processes absorb energy?

The answer lies in evaluating the change in entropy ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$) and applying the Gibbs free energy equation:

$$\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} - T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$$

1. Entropy Changes during Dissolution ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$)

Entropy ($S$) is a measure of the degree of thermal disorder or distribution of energy among particles in a system. When an ionic solid dissolves in water, two opposing entropy factors occur:

1. Solute Entropy Increase ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{solute}} > 0$)

The rigid, highly ordered 3D ionic crystal lattice breaks apart, releasing individual mobile ions that disperse randomly throughout the liquid solution. This dispersion dramatically increases microstates and disorder ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{solute}} \gg 0$).

2. Solvent Entropy Decrease ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{solvent}} < 0$)

Water molecules that were previously free to move randomly in bulk liquid become restricted and ordered as they align into fixed hydration shells around separated ions. This structural ordering decreases solvent entropy ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{solvent}} < 0$).

For the vast majority of simple ionic salts (e.g. $\mathrm{NaCl}$, $\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}$), the immense increase in solute disorder heavily outweighs the minor local ordering of water molecules. Thus, the net entropy change of dissolution is positive ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$).

Explaining Spontaneous Endothermic Dissolution

For an endothermic salt such as ammonium nitrate ($\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}$), $\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = +25.7\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

Despite $\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$ being energetically unfavourable, the large positive entropy change ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$) yields a large positive $T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$ term at room temperature ($298\text{ K}$).

Because $T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} > \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$, the net Gibbs free energy change is negative: $$\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} - T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} < 0$$ Thus, the dissolution process is thermodynamically spontaneous ($\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} < 0$) driven by entropy!

5. Saturated Solutions & Molecular Dynamic Equilibrium

Solution Equilibrium

When an ionic solid is continuously added to water, the concentration of dissolved ions increases. Eventually, a point is reached where no further solute appears to dissolve. This system is termed a saturated solution.

Definition of a Saturated Solution Equilibrium

A saturated solution is a dynamic equilibrium system established in a closed vessel (or vessel where solvent loss is prevented) at constant temperature, where undissolved solid ionic crystal coexists with dissolved aqueous ions: $$\mathrm{MX(s) \rightleftharpoons M^+(aq) + X^-(aq)}$$

Sub-microscopic Reversibility: Dissolution vs Recrystallisation

At the sub-microscopic level, a saturated solution is not static; it is in a state of dynamic equilibrium governed by two opposing kinetic rates:

  • Forward Process (Dissolution): Solid surface ions are dislodged by water collision and enter solution as hydrated ions ($\mathrm{MX(s) \to M^+(aq) + X^-(aq)}$).
  • Reverse Process (Recrystallisation / Precipitation): Hydrated ions in solution collide with the solid crystal surface, lose their hydration shell, and redeposit into the lattice ($\mathrm{M^+(aq) + X^-(aq) \to MX(s)}$).

When a solution becomes saturated, the rate of dissolution equals the rate of recrystallisation:

$$\text{Rate}_{\text{dissolution}} = \text{Rate}_{\text{recrystallisation}}$$

Because both processes occur simultaneously at identical rates, the macroscopic mass of undissolved solid and the molar concentrations of dissolved aqueous ions remain completely constant over time, fulfilling all criteria of dynamic equilibrium.

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Ion-Dipole Orientation Description

Describe the sub-microscopic orientation of water molecules as they form hydration shells around dissolved magnesium cations ($\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$) and nitrate anions ($\mathrm{NO_3^-}$).

Magnesium is a positively charged cation ($\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$). Polar water molecules align with their partially negative oxygen atoms ($\delta^-$) directed inwards towards the cation, forming strong ion-dipole attractive forces.

2. Hydration around $\mathrm{NO_3^-}$ Anion:
Nitrate is a negatively charged oxoanion ($\mathrm{NO_3^-}$). Polar water molecules align with their partially positive hydrogen atoms ($\delta^+$) directed inwards towards the anion, forming ion-dipole attractions.

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Calculating $\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$ using Hess's Law Cycle

3 Marks

The lattice dissociation enthalpy for potassium chloride ($\mathrm{KCl}$) is $\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}} = +715\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. The combined hydration enthalpy for gaseous potassium ions ($\mathrm{K^+}(g)$) and chloride ions ($\mathrm{Cl^-}(g)$) is $\Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}} = -698\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

(a) Calculate the molar enthalpy of solution ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$) for $\mathrm{KCl}$.
(b) State whether dissolving $\mathrm{KCl}$ in water causes the beaker temperature to increase or decrease.

Model Solution:

(a) Hess's Law Calculation: $$\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{hydration}}$$ $$\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = (+715\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) + (-698\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) = \mathbf{+17\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$$

(b) Temperature Change:
Because $\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = +17\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} > 0$, the dissolution of $\mathrm{KCl}$ is endothermic. Thermal energy is absorbed from the aqueous surroundings, causing the temperature of the beaker to decrease.

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Thermodynamic Justification of Spontaneous Endothermic Dissolution

4 Marks

When solid ammonium chloride ($\mathrm{NH_4Cl}$) dissolves in water at $298\text{ K}$, the process absorbs heat ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = +14.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$). Yet, $\mathrm{NH_4Cl}$ dissolves rapidly and spontaneously up to high concentrations.

Using the concepts of entropy ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$) and Gibbs free energy ($\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} - T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$), explain why this endothermic dissolution occurs spontaneously.

Model Solution:

1. Analysis of Enthalpy ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$):
Dissolution is endothermic ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = +14.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} > 0$), which is energetically unfavourable for spontaneity.

2. Analysis of Entropy ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$):
When solid $\mathrm{NH_4Cl(s)}$ dissolves, its rigid, highly ordered 3D crystalline lattice breaks apart, releasing free, mobile $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions into solution. This ion dispersion creates a massive increase in system disorder, resulting in a large positive entropy change ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$).

3. Gibbs Free Energy Evaluation ($\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}}$):
At $298\text{ K}$, the magnitude of the positive entropy term ($T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$) exceeds the positive enthalpy term ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$): $$T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} > \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$$ According to $\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} - T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$, this makes the overall Gibbs free energy change negative ($\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} < 0$). Therefore, dissolution is driven entirely by entropy and occurs spontaneously.

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Dynamic Equilibrium in Saturated Solutions & Temperature Dependence

5 Marks

A saturated solution of an endothermic salt ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$) coexists with excess undissolved solid at $25^\circ\text{C}$.

(a) Explain why this system is described as a dynamic equilibrium at the sub-microscopic level. (2 Marks)
(b) Predict and explain the effect on dissolved ion concentration when the temperature of the saturated mixture is increased to $50^\circ\text{C}$, referencing Le Chatelier's Principle. (3 Marks)

Model Solution:

(a) Sub-microscopic Dynamic Equilibrium:
At saturation, two opposing processes occur simultaneously at identical rates: $$\mathrm{MX(s) \rightleftharpoons M^+(aq) + X^-(aq)}$$ The rate at which surface ions detach from the crystal and become hydrated ($\text{Rate}_{\text{dissolution}}$) equals the rate at which aqueous ions collide with the solid lattice and recrystallise ($\text{Rate}_{\text{recrystallisation}}$). Because both rates are equal and non-zero, macroscopic concentrations remain completely constant.

(b) Temperature Shift Explanation:
Dissolution is endothermic ($\mathrm{MX(s) + heat \rightleftharpoons M^+(aq) + X^-(aq)}, \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$).
Increasing temperature adds thermal energy to the system. By Le Chatelier's Principle, the system shifts equilibrium position in the FORWARD direction ($\to$) to absorb the added heat.
This forward shift increases the concentration of dissolved aqueous ions ($[\mathrm{M^+}]$ and $[\mathrm{X^-}]$) while consuming undissolved solid $\mathrm{MX(s)}$, thereby increasing salt solubility at $50^\circ\text{C}$.

Question 3 (Hard): Entropy & Enthalpy Interplay in Dissolution

4 Marks

Account for the observation that calcium chloride ($\mathrm{CaCl_2}$) dissolves extremely exothermically ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = -81.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), whereas sodium nitrate ($\mathrm{NaNO_3}$) dissolves endothermically ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = +20.5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), yet both salts dissolve readily and spontaneously in water at room temperature.

Hint: Evaluate both enthalpy ($\Delta H$) and entropy ($\Delta S$) for each salt using $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. For $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$, $\Delta H < 0$ drives spontaneity. For $\mathrm{NaNO_3}$, $\Delta S > 0$ drives spontaneity.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
• Spontaneity is determined by the Gibbs free energy change: $\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} - T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}}$. Dissolution is spontaneous when $\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} < 0$.

• Calcium Chloride ($\mathrm{CaCl_2}$):
Dissolution is strongly exothermic ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = -81.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} < 0$) because the hydration enthalpy of small, highly charged $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ ions is extremely negative. The large negative $\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$ makes $\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}}$ negative, so dissolution is enthalpy-driven.

• Sodium Nitrate ($\mathrm{NaNO_3}$):
Dissolution is endothermic ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}} = +20.5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} > 0$), which opposes spontaneity. However, breaking the rigid crystal lattice releases free mobile ions, creating a large increase in system disorder ($\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} > 0$). At room temperature ($298\text{ K}$), $T\Delta S_{\mathrm{soln}} > \Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$, yielding $\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} < 0$. Thus, $\mathrm{NaNO_3}$ dissolution is entropy-driven.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Introduces Gibbs free energy formula $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ and criterion for spontaneity ($\Delta G < 0$).
  • 1 Mark: Explains that $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ is enthalpy-driven due to large negative $\Delta H_{\mathrm{soln}}$ from strong $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ ion hydration.
  • 1 Mark: Explains that $\mathrm{NaNO_3}$ is entropy-driven due to ion dispersion ($\Delta S > 0$) overcoming positive $\Delta H$.
  • 1 Mark: Concludes clearly that both achieve $\Delta G_{\mathrm{soln}} < 0$.

(b) Explain your prediction using the concept of dynamic equilibrium at the sub-microscopic level. (4 Marks)

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
(a) Prediction:
The undissolved solid $\mathrm{PbCl_2(s)}$ will become radioactive over time as radioactive $^{210}\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ ions are incorporated into the crystal lattice.

(b) Molecular Dynamic Equilibrium Explanation:
• Although the system appears static macroscopically (no change in total mass of solid or ion concentration), dissolution and recrystallisation continue perpetually at equal rates at the sub-microscopic level: $$\mathrm{PbCl_2(s) \rightleftharpoons Pb^{2+}(aq) + 2Cl^-(aq)}$$

• Simultaneously, non-radioactive $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ ions detach from the solid crystal lattice and enter solution at the exact same rate.
• This continuous exchange of ions between solid and aqueous phases proves that saturated solution equilibrium is dynamic rather than static.

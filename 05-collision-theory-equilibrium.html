1. Fundamental Postulates of Collision Theory in Reversible Systems

Microscopic Kinetics

In earlier lessons, we learned that dynamic equilibrium occurs in closed systems when the forward reaction rate equals the reverse reaction rate ($r_{\mathrm{forward}} = r_{\mathrm{reverse}} > 0$), resulting in constant macroscopic properties. To understand why and how this occurs at a molecular level, we must employ Collision Theory.

Collision theory states that for a chemical reaction to occur, reacting species must physically collide. However, the vast majority of collisions between particles in gas or solution phases are completely ineffective—particles simply bounce off one another elastically without altering their atomic structures.

A collision that successfully results in the breaking of existing chemical bonds and the formation of new chemical bonds is termed an effective (or successful) collision. For any collision between reactant or product species to be effective, it must satisfy three strict kinetic criteria simultaneously:

💥

1. Physical Collision ($Z$)

Reacting particles must physically collide in space. The total number of collisions occurring per unit volume per unit time is defined as the collision frequency ($Z$).

⚡

2. Activation Energy Threshold ($E \ge E_a$)

The colliding particles must possess a combined kinetic energy equal to or greater than the threshold activation energy ($E_a$) of the reaction ($E_{\mathrm{k}} \ge E_a$). This energy is necessary to overcome electrostatic electron-cloud repulsions and weaken existing bonds.

🎯

3. Correct Steric Orientation ($P$)

Particles must collide with a specific spatial orientation (steric factor, $P$) so that the reactive atomic centres align directly, permitting orbital overlap and transition state synthesis.

Symmetrical Application to Reversible Pathways

In a closed reversible reaction system, collision theory applies symmetrically to both forward and reverse pathways:
• Forward Pathway: Reactant particles collide with $E \ge E_{a,\text{f}}$ and correct steric orientation to form product molecules.
• Reverse Pathway: Product particles collide with $E \ge E_{a,\text{r}}$ and correct steric orientation to reform reactant molecules.
Because both forward and reverse collisions occur simultaneously in the same reaction vessel, the net chemical progression is governed entirely by the relative rates of effective forward and reverse collisions.

2. Potential Energy Profiles & Activation Energy ($E_a$) Architecture

Thermodynamic Profiles

The energetic pathway of a chemical reaction is represented visually using a Potential Energy Profile Diagram. This profile tracks the potential energy stored within chemical bonds as reacting species transform through a high-energy, short-lived arrangement known as the activated complex (or transition state, $\ddagger$).

For reversible reactions, two distinct activation energy barriers exist on the profile:
• Forward Activation Energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}$): The minimum energy required for reactants to reach the activated complex peak.
• Reverse Activation Energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$): The minimum energy required for products to reach the same activated complex peak.

NESA Potential Energy Profile for a Reversible Exothermic Reaction

Enthalpy ($\Delta H$) and Activation Energy Relationships

From the potential energy profile diagram, the mathematical connection between the enthalpy change ($\Delta H$), forward activation energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}$), and reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$) is defined by:

$$\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}}$$
Thermodynamic Classification Activation Energy Hierarchy Enthalpy Sign ($\Delta H$) Microscopic Barrier Consequence
Exothermic Reaction $E_{a,\text{f}} < E_{a,\text{r}}$ $\Delta H < 0$ (Negative) The reverse activation energy barrier is higher than the forward barrier. At equal temperatures, a smaller fraction of product collisions possess $E \ge E_{a,\text{r}}$ compared to reactant collisions exceeding $E_{a,\text{f}}$.
Endothermic Reaction $E_{a,\text{f}} > E_{a,\text{r}}$ $\Delta H > 0$ (Positive) The forward activation energy barrier is higher than the reverse barrier. Reactants require a higher minimum collision energy to overcome $E_{a,\text{f}}$ than products reforming reactants via $E_{a,\text{r}}$.

Nature of the Activated Complex ($\ddagger$)

The activated complex (or transition state, $\ddagger$) represents a maximum potential energy arrangement of atoms where reactant bonds are in the process of breaking while product bonds are simultaneously forming.
Because the activated complex sits at the energy peak, it is highly unstable and possesses an equal probability of collapsing backward to reform reactants or collapsing forward to form products.

3. Maxwell-Boltzmann Kinetic Energy Distributions

Statistical Thermodynamics

In any chemical system at a given temperature $T$, individual particles do not all move at the same speed. Random elastic collisions continually redistribute kinetic energy among particles. The spread of kinetic energies across the particle population is mathematically modelled by the Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution Curve.

Anatomy of the Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution Curve

  • Origin $(0,0)$: The curve begins at the origin because no particles possess zero kinetic energy at temperatures above absolute zero ($T > 0\text{ K}$).
  • Peak (Most Probable Energy, $E_{\mathrm{mp}}$): The peak represents the exact kinetic energy possessed by the largest single fraction of particles in the sample.
  • Average Energy ($E_{\mathrm{avg}}$): Because the curve is asymmetrical and skewed rightwards towards higher energies, the average kinetic energy ($E_{\mathrm{avg}}$) lies to the right of the peak ($E_{\mathrm{avg}} > E_{\mathrm{mp}}$).
  • Asymptotic High-Energy Tail: The curve approaches the horizontal axis at high kinetic energies but never touches it, reflecting a small but non-zero probability of finding extremely energetic particles.
  • Area Under the Curve: The total enclosed area beneath the curve represents the total number of particles present in the system.

Activation Energy Threshold ($E_a$) and Collision Fraction ($f$)

When an activation energy threshold ($E_a$) is marked on the energy axis, the area under the curve to the right of $E_a$ represents the fraction of particles possessing $E \ge E_a$.

NESA Maxwell-Boltzmann Energy Distribution for a Reversible Exothermic System

Temperature Effects on Collision Fractions

When temperature increases from $T_1$ to $T_2$ ($T_2 > T_1$):

  • The peak of the distribution shifts to the right (higher average kinetic energy) and flattens downwards (preserving total particle count/area).
  • The high-energy tail elevates significantly.
  • Consequently, the fraction of particles possessing kinetic energy exceeding both $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$ increases, accelerating both forward and reverse rates.

4. Molecular Orientation & The Steric Factor ($P$)

Collision Geometry

Even if colliding particles possess kinetic energy exceeding activation energy ($E \ge E_a$), the collision will still be ineffective if the particles are improperly aligned in three-dimensional space. The probability that a collision occurs with a favourable geometric orientation is defined as the steric factor ($P$).

Complex molecules with intricate shapes or localised reactive sites possess small steric factors ($P \ll 1$) because only a small fraction of collision geometries allow reactive atoms to contact directly. Simple monatomic ions or spherical atoms possess steric factors approaching unity ($P \approx 1$).

5. Microscopic Kinetic Walkthrough to Dynamic Equilibrium

Kinetic Progression

By combining collision frequency ($Z$), steric factor ($P$), and the fraction of collisions with $E \ge E_a$, we can trace how forward and reverse rates evolve over time in a sealed system.

Chronological Kinetic Evolution

Consider adding pure reactant species $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ to a sealed vessel at constant temperature $T$:

Stage 1: Initial Mixing ($t = 0$)

• Reactant concentrations $[\mathrm{A}]$ and $[\mathrm{B}]$ are at their maximum values.
• Collision frequency between reactants $Z_{\text{f}}$ is at its maximum peak, yielding the maximum forward reaction rate ($r_{\text{f}} = \text{Maximum}$).
• Zero product molecules exist ($[\mathrm{C}]=0$). Thus, reverse collision frequency $Z_{\text{r}} = 0$, making the reverse rate strictly zero ($r_{\text{r}} = 0$).

Stage 2: Approach to Equilibrium ($0 < t < t_{\text{eq}}$)

• As effective forward collisions transform reactants into products, $[\mathrm{A}]$ and $[\mathrm{B}]$ decrease.
• Declining reactant concentrations lower forward collision frequency ($Z_{\text{f}} \downarrow$), causing $r_{\text{f}}$ to decrease continuously.
• Concurrently, product molecules accumulate ($[\mathrm{C}] \uparrow$), increasing reverse collision frequency ($Z_{\text{r}} \uparrow$) and causing $r_{\text{r}}$ to rise from zero.

Stage 3: Dynamic Equilibrium ($t \ge t_{\text{eq}}$)

• Eventually, the declining forward rate $r_{\text{f}}$ and rising reverse rate $r_{\text{r}}$ become equal: $$r_{\text{f}} = r_{\text{r}} > 0$$ • Per unit time, the exact number of product molecules formed by forward collisions equals the number of product molecules converted back into reactants by reverse collisions.
• Macroscopic properties (colour, concentration, pressure) freeze while microscopic collisions continue indefinitely at equal non-zero rates.

7. Worked Examples (SCAFFOLDED NESA EXAM SOLUTIONS)

HSC Step-by-Step Solutions

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Identifying Collision Criteria for Reversibility

2 Marks

State the three criteria required for a collision between product particles to result in an effective reverse reaction, and explain why not all collisions between products reform reactants.

Model Solution:

1. Three Collision Criteria: Product particles must (i) physically collide, (ii) possess kinetic energy equal to or exceeding the reverse activation energy ($E \ge E_{a,\text{r}}$), and (iii) collide with correct steric orientation/molecular alignment.

2. Explanation: Most collisions are ineffective because colliding particles either lack sufficient kinetic energy to overcome $E_{a,\text{r}}$ or collide at improper steric angles, causing them to rebound elastically without breaking chemical bonds.

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Maxwell-Boltzmann Temperature Shift Analysis

3 Marks

Explain, with reference to Maxwell-Boltzmann kinetic energy distribution curves, why increasing the temperature of a closed reversible system increases the rates of both the forward and reverse reactions.

Model Solution:

1. Maxwell-Boltzmann Curve Shift: Raising temperature increases the average kinetic energy of all particles, causing the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve to flatten and its peak to shift rightwards toward higher energy values.

2. Fraction of Effective Collisions: As the distribution shifts rightward, the area under the curve beyond both the forward activation energy ($E \ge E_{a,\text{f}}$) and reverse activation energy ($E \ge E_{a,\text{r}}$) increases significantly.

3. Rate Impact: A larger fraction of both reactant and product collisions possess $E \ge E_a$, increasing the frequency of successful collisions per second in both directions. Consequently, both $r_{\text{f}}$ and $r_{\text{r}}$ increase.

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Quantitative Activation Energy & Enthalpy Profile Evaluation

4 Marks

For a generic gas-phase reversible reaction $\mathrm{X_2(g) + Y_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2XY(g)}$, the forward activation energy is $E_{a,\text{f}} = 72\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ and the reaction enthalpy is $\Delta H = -38\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
a) Calculate the reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$).
b) State whether the forward or reverse pathway has a higher kinetic energy barrier, justifying your answer using a potential energy relationship.

Model Solution:

a) Calculation:

$$\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}} \implies -38\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} = 72\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} - E_{a,\text{r}}$$ $$E_{a,\text{r}} = 72 - (-38) = 110\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$

b) Kinetic Barrier Evaluation: The reverse pathway possesses a higher activation energy barrier ($E_{a,\text{r}} = 110\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) than the forward pathway ($E_{a,\text{f}} = 72\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$). Because $\Delta H < 0$ (exothermic), products sit at a lower potential energy state than reactants, requiring product particles to absorb more energy to reach the transition state activated complex peak.

Worked Example 4 (HSC Exam Style): Integrated Stoichiometry & Reversible Collision Kinetics

5 Marks

A student investigates the reversible decomposition of gaseous nitrosyl chloride in a rigid $5.00\text{ L}$ sealed vessel at $250^\circ\text{C}$: $$\mathrm{2NOCl(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO(g) + Cl_2(g)}$$ The forward decomposition is endothermic with $\Delta H = +76.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ and forward activation energy $E_{a,\text{f}} = 98.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

a) Calculate the reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$) for the recombination of $\mathrm{NO(g)}$ and $\mathrm{Cl_2(g)}$. (2 Marks)
b) An initial sample of $0.400\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{NOCl(g)}$ is introduced into the vessel. At dynamic equilibrium, analysis reveals that $0.150\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{Cl_2(g)}$ has formed. Calculate the percentage decomposition of $\mathrm{NOCl(g)}$ and account for why the forward reaction rate decreased while the reverse reaction rate increased until equilibrium was reached. (3 Marks)

Model Solution:

a) Reverse Activation Energy Calculation:

$$\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}}$$ $$+76.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} = 98.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} - E_{a,\text{r}}$$ $$E_{a,\text{r}} = 98.0 - 76.0 = 22.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$

The reverse reaction has an activation energy threshold of $22.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

b) Stoichiometric Percentage Decomposition & Kinetic Explanation:

1. Stoichiometric Calculation: From the balanced equation, $1\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ formed corresponds to $2\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{NOCl}$ decomposed. $$\text{Moles of }\mathrm{NOCl}\text{ reacted} = 2 \times 0.150\text{ mol} = 0.300\text{ mol}$$ $$\text{Percentage decomposition} = \left(\frac{0.300\text{ mol}}{0.400\text{ mol}}\right) \times 100\% = 75.0\%$$

2. Collision Theory Account of Rates: • Forward Rate ($r_{\text{f}}$): As $\mathrm{NOCl}$ decomposes, $[\mathrm{NOCl}]$ decreases from $0.0800\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ to $0.0200\text{ mol L}^{-1}$. This reduction lowers the collision frequency between $\mathrm{NOCl}$ molecules ($Z_{\text{f}} \downarrow$), causing $r_{\text{f}}$ to decrease over time.
• Reverse Rate ($r_{\text{r}}$): Initially, $[\mathrm{NO}] = 0$ and $[\mathrm{Cl_2}] = 0$, so reverse collision frequency $Z_{\text{r}} = 0$. As products accumulate ($0.300\text{ mol NO}$ and $0.150\text{ mol Cl_2}$ formed), collisions between $\mathrm{NO}$ and $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ with $E \ge 22.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ become frequent ($Z_{\text{r}} \uparrow$), causing $r_{\text{r}}$ to rise from zero.
• Equilibrium: Net concentration changes cease when $r_{\text{f}} = r_{\text{r}} > 0$.

Question 1 (Easy): Activation Energy Barriers in Exothermic Systems

2 Marks

Contrast the magnitude of the forward activation energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}$) and reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$) for an exothermic reversible reaction, and state which pathway requires higher collision energy.

Hint: Recall $\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}}$. If $\Delta H < 0$, which $E_a$ must be larger?

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer: For an exothermic reaction ($\Delta H < 0$), the potential energy of products is lower than that of reactants. Consequently, the reverse activation energy barrier is greater than the forward activation energy barrier ($E_{a,\text{r}} > E_{a,\text{f}}$). Product particles require a higher minimum collision energy to reform reactants than reactant particles require to yield products.

Question 2 (Medium): Microscopic Catalyst Action on Reversible Collision Rates

3 Marks

Explain, using collision theory and energy profiles, why adding a catalyst increases the forward and reverse reaction rates equally without altering the position of dynamic equilibrium.

Hint: How does a catalyst affect the transition state peak height for forward vs reverse pathways?

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer: A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activated complex peak ($\ddagger$), lowering both $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$ by the exact same numerical quantity ($\Delta E_a$). According to Maxwell-Boltzmann distributions, lowering activation energy increases the fraction of particles possessing sufficient kinetic energy to react in both directions equally. Because $r_{\text{f}}$ and $r_{\text{r}}$ are accelerated by identical proportional factors, dynamic equilibrium is established faster without shifting relative reactant/product concentrations.

Question 3 (Hard): Kinetic Progression upon Volume Compression

4 Marks

An equilibrium mixture of gas-phase nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetroxide ($\mathrm{2NO_2(g) \rightleftharpoons N_2O_4(g)}$) is contained in a sealed syringe. The syringe volume is suddenly halved at constant temperature.
Analyse the immediate effect of this compression on molecular collision frequency ($Z_{\text{f}}$ vs $Z_{\text{r}}$) and explain microscopically how the system moves toward a new equilibrium state.

Hint: Look at the stoichiometry: $2\text{ moles of reactant gas} \rightleftharpoons 1\text{ mole of product gas}$. How does doubling concentration ($C = n/V$) affect bimolecular vs unimolecular collision frequency?

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer: Halving the volume doubles the concentration of all gas species ($C \propto 1/V$).
• Forward Rate ($r_{\text{f}}$): The forward reaction requires collision between two $\mathrm{NO_2}$ molecules. Doubling $[\mathrm{NO_2}]$ quadruples the collision frequency $Z_{\text{f}}$ between $\mathrm{NO_2}$ particles.
• Reverse Rate ($r_{\text{r}}$): The reverse reaction involves unimolecular decomposition of $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$. Doubling $[\mathrm{N_2O_4}]$ doubles the reverse collision frequency $Z_{\text{r}}$.
Because $Z_{\text{f}}$ quadruples while $Z_{\text{r}}$ only doubles, $r_{\text{f}} > r_{\text{r}}$ immediately after compression. More $\mathrm{NO_2}$ molecules collide and combine per second than $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$ molecules decompose, driving the reaction forward until $r_{\text{f}} = r_{\text{r}}$ is restored at a new equilibrium.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 4 Marks: Comprehensive microscopic analysis: identifies $C \propto 1/V$, contrasts 4x increase in bimolecular $Z_{\text{f}}$ with 2x increase in $Z_{\text{r}}$, deduces $r_{\text{f}} > r_{\text{r}}$, and explains progression to new equilibrium.
  • 2-3 Marks: Identifies collision frequency increases but fails to mathematically contrast bimolecular vs unimolecular rate dependencies.
  • 1 Mark: States that pressure increases collisions.

Question 4 (HSC Challenge): Collision Mechanics & Thermal Equilibrium Shifts

5 Marks

An endothermic reversible reaction $\mathrm{A_2(g) + B_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2AB(g)}$ ($\Delta H > 0$) is initially at dynamic equilibrium in a closed vessel. The temperature of the container is increased.
Synthesise collision frequency ($Z$), Maxwell-Boltzmann energy fractions, and activation energies ($E_{a,\text{f}}$ vs $E_{a,\text{r}}$) to explain why raising temperature causes the forward reaction rate to increase by a greater proportional factor than the reverse reaction rate, shifting the system to favour products.

Hint: Endothermic reaction $\implies E_{a,\text{f}} > E_{a,\text{r}}$. Connect Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution tail expansion to higher vs lower energy thresholds.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:

1. Activation Energy Hierarchy: Because the reaction is endothermic ($\Delta H > 0$), the forward activation energy is greater than the reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{f}} > E_{a,\text{r}}$).

2. Maxwell-Boltzmann Tail Expansion: Raising the temperature shifts the Maxwell-Boltzmann kinetic energy distribution curve to the right and flattens its peak, expanding the population of particles in the high-energy tail.

3. Relative Fractional Increase: Because $E_{a,\text{f}}$ sits further out along the high-energy tail than $E_{a,\text{r}}$, the proportional increase in the fraction of particles gaining energy $E \ge E_{a,\text{f}}$ is significantly greater than the proportional increase in particles gaining $E \ge E_{a,\text{r}}$.

4. Kinetic Outcome: Although collision frequency $Z$ rises for all species, the forward rate $r_{\text{f}}$ accelerates more than the reverse rate $r_{\text{r}}$ ($r_{\text{f}} > r_{\text{r}}$). Net forward reaction occurs until reactant consumption and product accumulation re-equalise rates ($r_{\text{f}} = r_{\text{r}}$) at a higher product yield.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 5 Marks: Complete rigorous synthesis: establishes $E_{a,\text{f}} > E_{a,\text{r}}$ for endothermic systems, uses Maxwell-Boltzmann tail expansion to explain greater proportional increase for higher $E_a$, deduces $r_{\text{f}} > r_{\text{r}}$, and details progression to higher product yield.
  • 3-4 Marks: Correctly identifies $E_{a,\text{f}} > E_{a,\text{r}}$ and states forward rate increases more, but lacks Maxwell-Boltzmann tail justification.
  • 1-2 Marks: Basic statement that heating favours endothermic reactions without collision theory depth.

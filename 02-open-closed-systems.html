LESSON 02 • PHASE A

Open vs Closed Systems

Analysing system boundaries, energy transfer, matter exchange, Aboriginal cooking processes, and their fundamental role in establishing dynamic equilibrium.

1. Understanding System Boundaries

Thermodynamic Foundations

In physical chemistry, a system is defined as the specific region of space or amount of matter under investigation (such as a conical flask containing reacting chemicals). Everything outside this boundary is designated as the surroundings. The nature of the boundary separating the system from its surroundings determines whether matter, thermal energy, or work can cross between them.

🔓

Open Systems

Exchanges both matter and energy with surroundings.

  • Gases, vapours, or dissolved ions can enter or leave freely.
  • Heat ($q$) or radiation moves across the boundary.
  • Equilibrium Status: Cannot establish dynamic equilibrium if gaseous products escape.
  • Example: An open beaker of boiling water, burning magnesium, or calcium carbonate heated in an open crucible.
🔒

Closed Systems

Exchanges energy, but NOT matter with surroundings.

  • Total mass inside the system remains strictly constant ($\Delta m = 0$).
  • Heat transfer (exothermic/endothermic) can still occur freely.
  • Equilibrium Status: Essential requirement for reversible reactions involving gases.
  • Example: A stoppered flask containing liquid water and water vapour, or a sealed soda bottle.
🛡️

Isolated Systems

Exchanges NEITHER energy NOR matter with surroundings.

  • No mass loss or gain ($\Delta m = 0$).
  • No thermal transfer across insulated boundaries ($\Delta q = 0$).
  • Equilibrium Status: Establishes static or dynamic internal equilibrium under total thermodynamic isolation.
  • Example: A perfectly insulated bomb calorimeter or thermos flask over short timeframes.

Cultural Application: Aboriginal Cooking & System Boundaries

Aboriginal Peoples of Australia have utilised principles of open and closed thermal systems for thousands of years in traditional food preparation:

  • Open Systems (Campfire Coals & Grilling): Cooking food directly over open campfire coals allows heat transfer to the food while water vapour, volatile aromatic oils, and combustion smoke escape freely into the surrounding atmosphere. This open system drives rapid dehydration and surface searing.
  • Closed Systems (Enclosed Earth/Pit Ovens - Kup-Murri): In pit oven cooking, a pit is dug into the earth, lined with fire-heated stones, filled with food wrapped in non-toxic leaves (such as paperbark or banana leaves), and completely covered with soil. The earth and leaf barrier acts as a closed system boundary that traps steam and moisture ($\Delta m = 0$) while retaining thermal energy. The sealed environment creates a high-humidity thermal equilibrium chamber that steams food evenly without charring or moisture loss.

The Core Rule for Dynamic Equilibrium

For any chemical system involving gases or volatile liquids, dynamic chemical equilibrium can ONLY be established and maintained in a closed system. If gaseous products escape into an open atmosphere, their effective concentration drops toward zero, preventing the reverse collision rate ($r_{\mathrm{reverse}}$) from matching the forward rate ($r_{\mathrm{forward}}$).

3. Microscopic & Quantitative Analysis of Open vs Closed Systems

Collision Dynamics & Mass Loss

To understand why dynamic equilibrium can only exist in a closed system, we must examine chemical collision frequencies and quantitative mass loss profiles.

In a Closed System ($\Delta m = 0$)

Consider the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate in a sealed, rigid vessel:

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \rightleftharpoons CaO(s) + CO_2(g)}$$
  • Initially, heating causes $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ to decompose at a forward rate $r_{\mathrm{forward}}$.
  • Gaseous $\mathrm{CO_2}$ molecules are retained within the fixed vessel volume.
  • As $[\mathrm{CO_2}]$ increases, collisions between gaseous $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and solid $\mathrm{CaO}$ become increasingly frequent.
  • The reverse reaction rate increases ($r_{\mathrm{reverse}} \uparrow$) until $r_{\mathrm{forward}} = r_{\mathrm{reverse}} > 0$.
  • A state of dynamic equilibrium is established. Total system mass remains $100\%$ constant.

In an Open System ($\Delta m < 0$)

Consider the exact same reaction occurring in an open crucible:

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow CaO(s) + CO_2(g)\uparrow}$$
  • Generated $\mathrm{CO_2}$ gas diffuses rapidly into the vast room atmosphere.
  • The concentration of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ above the solid mixture remains near zero ($[\mathrm{CO_2}] \to 0$).
  • Without gaseous $\mathrm{CO_2}$ molecules present, reverse collisions cannot occur ($r_{\mathrm{reverse}} \to 0$).
  • Because $r_{\mathrm{forward}} > 0$ and $r_{\mathrm{reverse}} \approx 0$, the reaction is driven continuously to the right until all $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ is consumed (going to completion).

4. NESA Worked Examples (HSC Exam Style)

Model Answers
Worked Example 1: Classifying System Boundaries Easy • 2 Marks

Question: Classify each of the following physical scenarios as an open, closed, or isolated system, and justify your answer:
a) Boiling water in an uncovered saucepan on an electric stove top.
b) A stoppered glass flask containing a mixture of liquid ethanol and ethanol vapour immersed in a water bath.

Step 1: Classify and justify Scenario (a)

Open System: Steam (water vapour/matter) escapes freely into the atmosphere, while thermal energy (heat) is continuously transferred from the stove through the saucepan walls to the surroundings.

Step 2: Classify and justify Scenario (b)

Closed System: The rubber stopper prevents ethanol molecules in both liquid and vapour phases from escaping, keeping total mass strictly constant ($\Delta m = 0$). However, thermal energy (heat) readily transfers across the glass walls between the water bath and the system.

Worked Example 2: Mass Loss Curve Analysis Medium • 3 Marks

Question: A student reacts excess dilute hydrochloric acid ($\mathrm{HCl}$) with marble chips ($\mathrm{CaCO_3}$) on an electronic balance under two conditions:
• Flask 1: An open conical flask.
• Flask 2: A sealed flask fitted with a rubber stopper.
Describe and account for the balance reading over time for Flask 1 compared to Flask 2.

Step 1: Write the balanced chemical equation $$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)\uparrow}$$
Step 2: Describe and account for Flask 1 (Open System)

In Flask 1, the mass reading decreases steadily over time as carbon dioxide gas ($\mathrm{CO_2}$) escapes from the open vessel into the atmosphere. The mass plateau indicates the reaction has reached completion upon consumption of $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$.

Step 3: Describe and account for Flask 2 (Closed System)

In Flask 2, the mass reading remains completely constant throughout the experiment ($\Delta m = 0$) because all produced $\mathrm{CO_2}$ gas is trapped inside, satisfying mass conservation for a closed system boundary.

Worked Example 3: Quantitative Mass Loss & Gas Volume Calculations Hard • 4 Marks

Question: A $25.00\text{ g}$ sample of pure $\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)}$ is placed in a flask with an initial total system mass of $250.00\text{ g}$ and heated to $800^\circ\text{C}$. $$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \rightleftharpoons CaO(s) + CO_2(g)}$$
a) Calculate the final total mass recorded on the balance if the reaction occurs in an open vessel and goes to completion.
b) Calculate the volume of $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ produced in part (a) measured at SATP ($25^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\text{ kPa}$, where $V_m = 24.79\text{ L/mol}$).

Step 1: Calculate moles of reactant $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ $$M(\mathrm{CaCO_3}) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09\text{ g/mol}$$ $$n(\mathrm{CaCO_3}) = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{25.00\text{ g}}{100.09\text{ g/mol}} = 0.24978\text{ mol}$$
Step 2: Calculate mass of escaping $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ and final system mass $$\text{By 1:1 stoichiometry, } n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.24978\text{ mol}$$ $$m(\mathrm{CO_2}) = n \times M = 0.24978\text{ mol} \times 44.01\text{ g/mol} = 10.993\text{ g}$$ $$\text{Final Mass (Open Vessel)} = 250.00\text{ g} - 10.993\text{ g} = 239.01\text{ g}$$
Step 3: Calculate volume of gas produced at SATP $$V(\mathrm{CO_2}) = n \times V_m = 0.24978\text{ mol} \times 24.79\text{ L/mol} = 6.19\text{ L}$$
Worked Example 4: Analysing System Boundaries in Aboriginal Earth Ovens and Industrial Equilibrium HSC Advanced • 5 Marks

Question: Compare the thermodynamic behavior of heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate ($\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O}$) in an open crucible versus a sealed glass tube, and evaluate how Aboriginal earth ovens (Kup-Murri) exploit closed system boundaries to control water vapour loss during food preparation.

Step 1: Analyse open crucible heating ($\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O}$)

In an open crucible, heating decomposes blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate into white anhydrous solid and steam: $\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O(s) \rightarrow CuSO_4(s) + 5H_2O(g)\uparrow}$. Steam escapes freely into the open environment ($\Delta m < 0$). Because $[\mathrm{H_2O(g)}]$ above the solid remains zero, reverse collisions cannot occur ($r_{\mathrm{reverse}} = 0$), driving the reaction to complete dehydration.

Step 2: Analyse sealed tube heating ($\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O}$)

In a sealed glass tube (closed system), released water vapour is retained ($\Delta m = 0$). As $[\mathrm{H_2O(g)}]$ builds inside the headspace, the rate of reverse hydration collisions between $\mathrm{CuSO_4(s)}$ and $\mathrm{H_2O(g)}$ increases until $r_{\mathrm{forward}} = r_{\mathrm{reverse}}$, establishing dynamic equilibrium where blue hydrated and white anhydrous crystals coexist.

Step 3: Connect to Aboriginal pit oven technology

Similarly, Aboriginal earth ovens (Kup-Murri) wrap food in leaves and seal it under soil. This closed system boundary retains water vapour released during heating ($\Delta m = 0$). By trapping steam in dynamic equilibrium with moisture in the food, thermal energy is transferred efficiently while preventing dehydration, producing evenly cooked, moist food.

Question 2: Collision Theory in Open System Decomposition

3 Marks

Explain, using collision theory, why heating copper(II) carbonate ($\mathrm{CuCO_3(s) \rightarrow CuO(s) + CO_2(g)}$) in an open test tube fails to establish dynamic equilibrium.

Hint: What happens to $[\mathrm{CO_2}]$ in an open vessel and how does this affect reverse collisions?

Model Answer & Rubric

1. Matter Loss: In an open test tube, generated $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$ escapes into the surrounding atmosphere.

2. Reverse Collision Rate: The loss of gas keeps $[\mathrm{CO_2}]$ inside the tube near zero, reducing reverse collisions between $\mathrm{CaO}$ and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ to near zero ($r_{\mathrm{reverse}} \approx 0$).

3. Result: Because $r_{\mathrm{forward}} > r_{\mathrm{reverse}}$, the forward reaction continues until $\mathrm{CuCO_3}$ is completely consumed (going to completion).

Question 3: Quantitative Decomposition in Open vs Closed Systems

4 Marks

Thermal decomposition of sodium hydrogen carbonate occurs via: $$\mathrm{2NaHCO_3(s) \rightleftharpoons Na_2CO_3(s) + H_2O(g) + CO_2(g)}$$ A sample of $16.80\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{NaHCO_3(s)}$ is heated to $200^\circ\text{C}$ in an open vessel and decomposes completely.
a) Calculate the total mass of gaseous products released into the surroundings.
b) Calculate the total volume of gas ($\mathrm{H_2O + CO_2}$) produced at STP ($0^\circ\text{C}, 100\text{ kPa}$ where $V_m = 22.71\text{ L/mol}$).

Hint: Calculate $n(\mathrm{NaHCO_3})$, use stoichiometric ratios ($2:1:1$), then calculate masses and total gas moles.

Model Answer & Rubric

a) Mass of Gaseous Products:

$$M(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}) = 22.99 + 1.008 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 84.008\text{ g/mol}$$ $$n(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}) = \frac{16.80\text{ g}}{84.008\text{ g/mol}} = 0.200\text{ mol}$$ $$\text{By 2:1 stoichiometry: } n(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 0.100\text{ mol}, \quad n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.100\text{ mol}$$ $$m(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 0.100 \times 18.016 = 1.802\text{ g}$$ $$m(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.100 \times 44.01 = 4.401\text{ g}$$ $$\text{Total Gas Mass Released} = 1.802 + 4.401 = 6.20\text{ g}$$

b) Total Gas Volume at STP:

$$n_{\text{total gas}} = n(\mathrm{H_2O}) + n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.100 + 0.100 = 0.200\text{ mol}$$ $$V_{\text{total}} = n_{\text{total}} \times V_m = 0.200\text{ mol} \times 22.71\text{ L/mol} = 4.54\text{ L}$$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates moles of $\mathrm{NaHCO_3}$ ($0.200\text{ mol}$).
  • 1 Mark: Applies stoichiometry to find moles of gas products.
  • 1 Mark: Calculates total mass of escaping gas ($6.20\text{ g}$).
  • 1 Mark: Calculates total volume of gas at STP ($4.54\text{ L}$).

Question 4: Practical Investigation of Open vs Closed System Reversibility

5 Marks

A student designs an experiment to investigate the reversibility of copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate decomposition ($\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O(s) \rightleftharpoons CuSO_4(s) + 5H_2O(g)}$).
Outline a two-part practical procedure (Part A: Open System, Part B: Closed System) and explain how mass measurements and visual observations demonstrate the necessity of a closed system for establishing dynamic equilibrium.

Hint: Describe open crucible heating (mass loss, blue to white) vs sealed glass tube heating (constant mass, condensation, coexistence).

Model Answer & Rubric

Experimental Procedure:

Part A (Open System): Heat a weighed sample of blue $\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O(s)}$ in an open porcelain crucible over a Bunsen burner. Record mass before and after heating.
Observations & Deduction: The solid turns white as steam escapes. The mass decreases significantly ($\Delta m < 0$). Escaping steam prevents reverse hydration collisions, driving the reaction to complete dehydration.

Part B (Closed System): Heat blue $\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O(s)}$ inside a sealed glass boiling tube. Record total tube mass throughout.
Observations & Deduction: Water vapour condenses on the cool upper walls inside the tube while the solid partially turns white. Total mass remains $100\%$ constant ($\Delta m = 0$). Upon standing, condensed water re-reacts with the white solid to reform blue hydrated crystals, establishing dynamic equilibrium.

Conclusion: Demonstrates that chemical reversibility requires a closed system boundary to retain gaseous components and enable equal forward and reverse rates.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Outlines a valid procedure for Part A (open crucible) and Part B (sealed tube).
  • 1 Mark: Includes expected color changes (blue $\rightleftharpoons$ white).
  • 1 Mark: Connects open system mass loss ($\Delta m < 0$) to complete dehydration.
  • 1 Mark: Connects closed system mass conservation ($\Delta m = 0$) to steam retention.
  • 1 Mark: Evaluates how closed boundaries enable reverse collisions required for dynamic equilibrium.

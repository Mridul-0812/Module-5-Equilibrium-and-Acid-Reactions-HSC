1. The Reaction Quotient ($Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$) for Heterogeneous Dissolution

Thermodynamic State Variable

In Phase C (Lesson 14), we defined the general Reaction Quotient ($Q$) as a mathematical expression showing the ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations at any arbitrary instantaneous moment during a chemical reaction, whether at equilibrium or not.

When applied to the heterogeneous dissolution of a sparingly soluble ionic salt $A_x B_{y(s)}$: $$A_x B_{y(s)} \rightleftharpoons x A^{m+}_{(aq)} + y B^{n-}_{(aq)}$$ the Solubility Reaction Quotient (commonly designated as $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ or simply $Q$) is formulated using the exact same stoichiometric algebraic expression as $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$:

Mathematical Definition of $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$

$$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [A^{m+}]_i^x [B^{n-}]_i^y$$

where $[A^{m+}]_i$ and $[B^{n-}]_i$ represent the INSTANTANEOUS, INITIAL DILUTED CONCENTRATIONS ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$) of the constituent ions present in the mixture immediately upon combining solutions, before any precipitation or dissolution has taken place.

Key Distinction: $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ versus $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$

Understanding the fundamental thermodynamic distinction between $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is paramount for HSC examination success:

Property Solubility Product Constant ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}$) Solubility Reaction Quotient ($Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$)
Thermodynamic Nature Fixed thermodynamic constant for a specific salt at a given temperature. Variable state quantity that changes continuously as reaction progresses.
System State Requirement Applies ONLY when the solution is saturated and in true dynamic equilibrium with undissolved solid. Applies to ANY arbitrary non-equilibrium state, including initial mixing moments.
Concentrations Used Equilibrium concentrations $[A^{m+}]_{\mathrm{eq}}$ and $[B^{n-}]_{\mathrm{eq}}$. Initial diluted non-equilibrium concentrations $[A^{m+}]_i$ and $[B^{n-}]_i$.
Predictive Capability Represents the upper limit of dissolved ion activity before solid formation occurs. Compared against $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ to determine the direction of spontaneous shift.

2. The Three Thermodynamic States & Precipitation Verdicts

Core Decision Matrix

Comparing the numerical value of $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ calculated immediately after mixing against the published $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ constant yields one of three mutually exclusive thermodynamic outcomes:

1. Unsaturated Solution ($Q_{\mathrm{sp}} < K_{\mathrm{sp}}$)

• Condition: $[A^{m+}]_i^x [B^{n-}]_i^y < K_{\mathrm{sp}}$
• Physical State: The ion product is below the thermodynamic saturation limit.
• Precipitation Verdict: NO PRECIPITATE FORMS!
• System Behaviour: The solution remains a clear, homogenous liquid. If solid precipitate were added, it would spontaneously dissolve ($\to$) until $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.

2. Saturated Solution ($Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$)

• Condition: $[A^{m+}]_i^x [B^{n-}]_i^y = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$
• Physical State: The solution holds the maximum possible concentration of dissolved ions in dynamic equilibrium.
• Precipitation Verdict: AT EQUILIBRIUM (Threshold)
• System Behaviour: The solution is exactly saturated. No net precipitation occurs ($\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = \text{Rate}_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$).

3. Precipitating System ($Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$)

• Condition: $[A^{m+}]_i^x [B^{n-}]_i^y > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$
• Physical State: The solution is temporarily supersaturated and thermodynamically unstable.
• Precipitation Verdict: A PRECIPITATE FORMS!
• System Behaviour: Excess ions rapidly combine and crystallise out of solution, shifting the equilibrium to the LEFT ($\leftarrow$) until ion concentrations drop back down to satisfy $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.

3. The Dilution Effect on Mixing Solutions ($c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2$)

Essential Pre-Calculation Step

In HSC Chemistry examination questions, predicting precipitation almost always involves pouring two separate aqueous salt solutions together (for example, mixing $100\text{ mL}$ of aqueous $\mathrm{AgNO_3}$ with $400\text{ mL}$ of aqueous $\mathrm{NaCl}$).

THE #1 STUDENT ERROR IN MODULE 5 EXAMS!

NEVER substitute the starting stock solution concentrations directly into the $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression!

When two liquid solutions are mixed, the total liquid volume increases ($V_{\mathrm{total}} = V_1 + V_2$). Because volume increases, the initial concentration of every ion present in the mixture is immediately DILUTED before any precipitation reaction occurs!

Failing to calculate diluted concentrations before evaluating $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ results in an artificially inflated $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ value and zero marks awarded for the calculation!

The Dilution Formula for Mixing Solutions

For each individual solution being combined, apply the standard conservation of mass dilution equation:

$$c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2 \implies c_2 = \frac{c_1 V_1}{V_2} = \frac{c_1 V_1}{V_{\mathrm{total}}}$$

Step-by-Step Dilution Protocol

  1. 1. Calculate Total Solution Volume ($V_{\mathrm{total}}$): $$V_{\mathrm{total}} = V_1 + V_2$$ (Ensure volumes are in matching units, typically $\text{mL}$ or $\text{L}$).
  2. 2. Calculate Diluted Cation Concentration ($[A^{m+}]_i$): $$[A^{m+}]_i = \frac{c(\text{stock cation}) \times V(\text{cation solution})}{V_{\mathrm{total}}}$$
  3. 3. Calculate Diluted Anion Concentration ($[B^{n-}]_i$): $$[B^{n-}]_i = \frac{c(\text{stock anion}) \times V(\text{anion solution})}{V_{\mathrm{total}}}$$

4. Standardised NESA Algorithm for Precipitation Questions

Master Examination Scaffold

When responding to a NESA examination question asking whether a precipitate will form upon mixing solutions, structure your answer using the following 5-step master algorithm to guarantee full marks:

The 5-Step Precipitation Scaffold

Step 1: Identify Potential Precipitate & Write Equilibrium Equation

Identify the cation-anion pair that forms a sparingly soluble salt using solubility rules (Lesson 20). Write the balanced ionic dissociation equation: $$\mathrm{Salt_{(s)} \rightleftharpoons x Cation^{m+}_{(aq)} + y Anion^{n-}_{(aq)}}$$

Step 2: Calculate Diluted Ion Concentrations ($c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2$)

Determine $V_{\mathrm{total}} = V_1 + V_2$. Calculate the initial diluted concentrations $[A^{m+}]_i$ and $[B^{n-}]_i$ in the total mixture volume.

Step 3: State the $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Mathematical Expression

Formulate $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ matching the salt's stoichiometry: $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [A^{m+}]_i^x [B^{n-}]_i^y$$

Step 4: Substitute Diluted Concentrations & Calculate $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$

Substitute the diluted ion concentrations from Step 2 into the $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression and compute the numerical value using correct powers.

Step 5: Compare $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ with $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ & Write Final Verdict

State the explicit inequality comparison ($Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$, $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} < K_{\mathrm{sp}}$, or $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$) and give a definitive written conclusion:
"Since $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$, the system is supersaturated. A solid precipitate of [Formula] WILL form."

6. Scaffolded Worked Examples

HSC Step-by-Step Solutions

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Direct $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Evaluation in a Single Vessel

2 Marks

A laboratory solution is prepared such that the concentration of calcium ions is $[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ and the concentration of fluoride ions is $[\mathrm{F^-}] = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$.

Given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{CaF_2}) = 3.45 \times 10^{-11}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$, determine whether a precipitate of calcium fluoride ($\mathrm{CaF_2}$) will form.

Model Solution:

1. Write the equilibrium equation & $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression: $$\mathrm{CaF_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2F^-_{(aq)}}$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i [\mathrm{F^-}]_i^2$$

2. Substitute initial concentrations and calculate $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$: $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = (1.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (2.0 \times 10^{-4})^2$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = (1.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (4.0 \times 10^{-8}) = \mathbf{4.0 \times 10^{-11}}$$

3. Compare $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ with $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ & state verdict:
Since $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} (4.0 \times 10^{-11}) > K_{\mathrm{sp}} (3.45 \times 10^{-11})$, $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.
Verdict: The solution is supersaturated. A white precipitate of $\mathrm{CaF_2}$ **WILL form**. (2 Marks)

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Mixing Two Solutions ($1:1$ Salt $\mathrm{AgCl}$)

3 Marks

A student mixes $100.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.010\text{ M}$ silver nitrate solution ($\mathrm{AgNO_{3(aq)}}$) with $400.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.0050\text{ M}$ sodium chloride solution ($\mathrm{NaCl_{(aq)}}$).

Given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{AgCl}) = 1.77 \times 10^{-10}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$, perform a quantitative calculation to predict whether a precipitate of silver chloride ($\mathrm{AgCl}$) will form.

Model Solution:

1. Calculate total final volume $V_{\mathrm{total}}$: $$V_{\mathrm{total}} = 100.0\text{ mL} + 400.0\text{ mL} = 500.0\text{ mL} = 0.5000\text{ L}$$

2. Calculate diluted initial ion concentrations ($c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2$): $$[\mathrm{Ag^+}]_i = \frac{0.010\text{ M} \times 100.0\text{ mL}}{500.0\text{ mL}} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$ $$[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_i = \frac{0.0050\text{ M} \times 400.0\text{ mL}}{500.0\text{ mL}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$

3. Formulate $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and compute: $$\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)} \rightleftharpoons Ag^+_{(aq)} + Cl^-_{(aq)}}$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}]_i [\mathrm{Cl^-}]_i = (2.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (4.0 \times 10^{-3}) = \mathbf{8.0 \times 10^{-6}}$$

4. Compare $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ with $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ & conclusion:
Since $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} (8.0 \times 10^{-6}) \gg K_{\mathrm{sp}} (1.77 \times 10^{-10})$, $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.
Verdict: A white precipitate of solid $\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)}}$ **WILL form**. (3 Marks)

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Mixing Two Solutions ($1:2$ Salt $\mathrm{PbI_2}$)

4 Marks

A solution containing $150.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.020\text{ M}$ lead(II) nitrate ($\mathrm{Pb(NO_3)_2}$) is poured into $350.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.010\text{ M}$ potassium iodide ($\mathrm{KI}$).

Given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{PbI_2}) = 8.79 \times 10^{-9}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$, determine whether a yellow precipitate of lead(II) iodide ($\mathrm{PbI_2}$) will precipitate out of the mixture.

Model Solution:

1. Calculate total final volume $V_{\mathrm{total}}$: $$V_{\mathrm{total}} = 150.0\text{ mL} + 350.0\text{ mL} = 500.0\text{ mL} = 0.5000\text{ L}$$

2. Apply dilution formula ($c_2 = \frac{c_1 V_1}{V_{\mathrm{total}}}$): $$[\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}]_i = \frac{0.020\text{ M} \times 150.0\text{ mL}}{500.0\text{ mL}} = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$ $$[\mathrm{I^-}]_i = \frac{0.010\text{ M} \times 350.0\text{ mL}}{500.0\text{ mL}} = 7.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$

3. Write balanced dissociation equation & $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ formula: $$\mathrm{PbI_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons Pb^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2I^-_{(aq)}}$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}]_i [\mathrm{I^-}]_i^2$$

4. Substitute diluted values and compute $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$: $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = (6.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (7.0 \times 10^{-3})^2$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = (6.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (4.9 \times 10^{-5}) = \mathbf{2.94 \times 10^{-7}}$$

5. Compare $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ with $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ & state verdict:
Since $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} (2.94 \times 10^{-7}) > K_{\mathrm{sp}} (8.79 \times 10^{-9})$, $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.
Verdict: The solution is supersaturated. A bright yellow precipitate of $\mathrm{PbI_{2(s)}}$ **WILL form**. (4 Marks)

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Calculating Threshold $\mathrm{pH}$ for Selective Precipitation

5 Marks

An industrial wastewater sample contains iron(III) ions at a concentration of $[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}] = 0.050\text{ mol L}^{-1}$.

Given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{Fe(OH)_3}) = 2.79 \times 10^{-39}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $K_{\mathrm{w}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$:
(a) Calculate the minimum concentration of hydroxide ions $[\mathrm{OH^-}]$ required to initiate the precipitation of solid $\mathrm{Fe(OH)_3}$. (3 Marks)
(b) Determine the minimum threshold $\mathrm{pH}$ above which $\mathrm{Fe(OH)_3}$ begins precipitating. (2 Marks)

Model Solution:

(a) Minimum Hydroxide Ion Concentration $[\mathrm{OH^-}]_{\mathrm{min}}$:
1. Precipitation begins exactly at the boundary where $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$: $$\mathrm{Fe(OH)_{3(s)} \rightleftharpoons Fe^{3+}_{(aq)} + 3OH^-_{(aq)}}$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}][\mathrm{OH^-}]^3 = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$$ 2. Substitute $[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}] = 0.050\text{ M}$ and $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 2.79 \times 10^{-39}$: $$(0.050) \times [\mathrm{OH^-}]^3 = 2.79 \times 10^{-39}$$ $$[\mathrm{OH^-}]^3 = \frac{2.79 \times 10^{-39}}{0.050} = 5.58 \times 10^{-38}$$ $$[\mathrm{OH^-}] = \sqrt[3]{5.58 \times 10^{-38}} = \mathbf{3.82 \times 10^{-13}\text{ mol L}^{-1}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

(b) Threshold $\mathrm{pH}$ Calculation:
1. Calculate $\mathrm{pOH}$: $$\mathrm{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{OH^-}] = -\log_{10}(3.82 \times 10^{-13}) = 12.418$$ 2. Calculate $\mathrm{pH}$: $$\mathrm{pH} = 14.000 - \mathrm{pOH} = 14.000 - 12.418 = \mathbf{1.58}$$
Conclusion: Iron(III) hydroxide is so extremely insoluble that it begins precipitating out of solution at any $\mathrm{pH}$ above **1.58** (even in strongly acidic solutions!).

Question 1 (Easy): Explaining the $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ vs $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Condition

State the mathematical condition involving $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ under which a solid precipitate will spontaneously form when two solutions are mixed, and explain the physical meaning of this condition.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
• A solid precipitate will form when **$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$**.
• This condition indicates that the product of the initial diluted ion concentrations exceeds the equilibrium saturation constant ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}$). The solution is thermodynamically supersaturated and unstable, driving the reaction in the reverse direction ($\leftarrow$) to precipitate solid salt until $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.

Question 2 (Medium): Predicting Barium Sulfate Precipitation

3 Marks

A student combines $200.0\text{ mL}$ of $1.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}$ barium nitrate solution ($\mathrm{Ba(NO_3)_2}$) with $300.0\text{ mL}$ of $2.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}$ sodium sulfate solution ($\mathrm{Na_2SO_4}$).

Given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{BaSO_4}) = 1.08 \times 10^{-10}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$, perform a calculation to determine whether a precipitate of barium sulfate ($\mathrm{BaSO_4}$) will form.

Hint: Total volume $V_{\mathrm{total}} = 500.0\text{ mL}$. Calculate diluted $[\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}]$ and $[\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]$ first!

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
1. Total Volume $V_{\mathrm{total}} = 200.0 + 300.0 = 500.0\text{ mL}$.

2. Calculate diluted initial concentrations: $$[\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}]_i = \frac{(1.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}) \times 200.0\text{ mL}}{500.0\text{ mL}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$ $$[\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]_i = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}) \times 300.0\text{ mL}}{500.0\text{ mL}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$
3. Calculate $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$: $$\mathrm{BaSO_{4(s)} \rightleftharpoons Ba^{2+}_{(aq)} + SO_{4(aq)}^{2-}}$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}]_i [\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]_i = (4.0 \times 10^{-6}) \times (1.2 \times 10^{-5}) = \mathbf{4.8 \times 10^{-11}}$$
4. Compare $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ with $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$:
Since $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} (4.8 \times 10^{-11}) < K_{\mathrm{sp}} (1.08 \times 10^{-10})$, $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} < K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.
Verdict: The solution remains unsaturated. **NO PRECIPITATE FORMS**.

  • 1 Mark: Calculates $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = 4.8 \times 10^{-11}$.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly compares $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} < K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and concludes no precipitate forms.

Question 3 (Hard): Calcium Fluoride Mixing with Power Term

4 Marks

A laboratory technician mixes $250.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.0080\text{ M}$ calcium chloride solution ($\mathrm{CaCl_2}$) with $250.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.0040\text{ M}$ sodium fluoride solution ($\mathrm{NaF}$).

Given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{CaF_2}) = 3.45 \times 10^{-11}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$, evaluate $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and state whether a precipitate of calcium fluoride ($\mathrm{CaF_2}$) will form.

Hint: Mixing equal volumes ($250\text{ mL} + 250\text{ mL} = 500\text{ mL}$) halves both initial concentrations! Don't forget to square $[\mathrm{F^-}]$ in $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$!

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
1. Total Volume $V_{\mathrm{total}} = 250.0 + 250.0 = 500.0\text{ mL}$ (volume doubled, concentrations halved).

2. Calculate diluted initial concentrations: $$[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i = \frac{0.0080\text{ M}}{2} = 0.0040\text{ mol L}^{-1} = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$ $$[\mathrm{F^-}]_i = \frac{0.0040\text{ M}}{2} = 0.0020\text{ mol L}^{-1} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$

Formulate $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and compute: $$\mathrm{CaF_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2F^-_{(aq)}}$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i [\mathrm{F^-}]_i^2$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = (4.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3})^2$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = (4.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (4.0 \times 10^{-6}) = \mathbf{1.6 \times 10^{-8}}$$
4. Compare $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ with $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$:
Since $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} (1.6 \times 10^{-8}) \gg K_{\mathrm{sp}} (3.45 \times 10^{-11})$, $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.
Verdict: A white precipitate of solid $\mathrm{CaF_{2(s)}}$ **WILL form**.

Question 4 (Extension): Threshold Addition & Precipitate Mass Calculation

5 Marks

A $500.0\text{ mL}$ sample of $0.020\text{ M}$ lead(II) nitrate solution ($\mathrm{Pb(NO_3)_2}$) is maintained at $25^\circ\text{C}$.

Given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{PbSO_4}) = 2.53 \times 10^{-8}$ and $M(\mathrm{PbSO_4}) = 303.26\text{ g mol}^{-1}$:
(a) Calculate the minimum concentration of sulfate ions $[\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]$ required to begin precipitating lead(II) sulfate ($\mathrm{PbSO_4}$). (2 Marks)
(b) If $0.050\text{ moles}$ of solid sodium sulfate ($\mathrm{Na_2SO_4}$) is dissolved into this solution without changing its $500.0\text{ mL}$ volume, calculate the mass of $\mathrm{PbSO_4}$ precipitate formed at equilibrium. (3 Marks)

Hint: Part (a): Set $Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$. Part (b): Identify limiting reagent between $n(\mathrm{Pb^{2+}})$ and $n(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}})$.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
(a) Threshold Concentration $[\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]_{\mathrm{min}}$: $$\mathrm{PbSO_{4(s)} \rightleftharpoons Pb^{2+}_{(aq)} + SO_{4(aq)}^{2-}}$$ $$Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}][\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}] = K_{\mathrm{sp}}$$ $$(0.020\text{ M}) \times [\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}] = 2.53 \times 10^{-8}$$ $$[\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]_{\mathrm{min}} = \frac{2.53 \times 10^{-8}}{0.020} = \mathbf{1.27 \times 10^{-6}\text{ mol L}^{-1}}$$
(b) Mass of Precipitate Formed:
1. Calculate initial moles of each ion: $$n(\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}) = c \times V = 0.020\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 0.5000\text{ L} = 0.010\text{ mol}$$ $$n(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}) = 0.050\text{ mol}$$
2. Identify limiting reagent:
Stoichiometry is $1:1$. Since $0.010\text{ mol } \mathrm{Pb^{2+}} < 0.050\text{ mol } \mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$, $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ is the **limiting reagent**.

3. Because $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is extremely small ($10^{-8}$), virtually ALL $0.010\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ precipitates as solid $\mathrm{PbSO_4}$: $$n(\mathrm{PbSO_{4(s)}}) \approx 0.010\text{ mol}$$
4. Calculate mass of precipitate $m$: $$m = n \times M = 0.010\text{ mol} \times 303.26\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{3.03\text{ g}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

  • 1 Mark: Solves $[\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]_{\mathrm{min}} = 1.27 \times 10^{-6}\text{ M}$.
  • 1 Mark: Calculates initial moles $n(\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}) = 0.010\text{ mol}$ and identifies $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ as limiting reagent.
  • 1 Mark: Deduces $0.010\text{ mol}$ of precipitate is formed.

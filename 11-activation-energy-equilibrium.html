1. Fundamental Energetics: Enthalpy ($\Delta H$) and Reversibility

Core Thermochemistry

Every chemical species possesses a distinct quantity of chemical potential energy stored within its intra- and intermolecular chemical bonds, known as enthalpy ($H$). While absolute enthalpy cannot be measured directly, the overall change in enthalpy ($\Delta H$, or heat of reaction) during a chemical transformation is readily determined calorimetrically by monitoring energy transfer between the system and its surroundings at constant pressure.

For any chemical reaction proceeding from initial reactants to final products, the standard enthalpy change is defined as:

$$\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}$$

In a reversible system ($\mathrm{A + B \rightleftharpoons C + D}$), both the forward pathway ($\to$) and the reverse pathway ($\leftarrow$) occur concurrently within the same reaction vessel. This introduces a fundamental thermodynamic constraint:

The Thermodynamic Reversibility Principle

If the forward reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H_{\text{forward}} < 0$), energy is released into the surroundings because $H_{\text{products}} < H_{\text{reactants}}$. Consequently, the reverse reaction MUST be endothermic ($\Delta H_{\text{reverse}} > 0$) with the exact same numerical magnitude: $$\Delta H_{\text{reverse}} = -\Delta H_{\text{forward}}$$
Conversely, if the forward pathway is endothermic ($\Delta H_{\text{forward}} > 0$), the reverse pathway is exothermic ($\Delta H_{\text{reverse}} < 0$).

2. Reversible Energy Profile Diagrams (Potential Energy Surfaces)

Graphical Framework

A reaction profile diagram (or potential energy diagram) plots the potential energy ($H$) of a chemical system against the reaction coordinate (a non-linear metric representing the geometric progress of bond breaking and bond formation from reactants to products).

Unlike irreversible reactions—which are read strictly left-to-right—a reversible reaction profile MUST be interpreted bidirectionally. Key landmark features on these surfaces include:

  • Reactant Enthalpy Level ($H_{\text{reactants}}$): The baseline potential energy plateau of the forward reactants.
  • Product Enthalpy Level ($H_{\text{products}}$): The baseline potential energy plateau of the forward products (reverse reactants).
  • Transition State / Activated Complex ($\text{TS}^\ddagger$): The potential energy maximum along the reaction coordinate representing the highest energy molecular configuration during collision.
  • Forward Activation Energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}$): The minimum energy bump that reactant molecules must overcome to form the transition state: $$E_{a,\text{f}} = H_{\text{TS}} - H_{\text{reactants}}$$
  • Reverse Activation Energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$): The energy barrier that product molecules must overcome to re-form reactants: $$E_{a,\text{r}} = H_{\text{TS}} - H_{\text{products}}$$

Figure 11.2: Reversible Endothermic Reaction Profile ($\Delta H_{\text{forward}} > 0$) Showing $E_{a,\text{f}}$, $E_{a,\text{r}}$, and $\Delta H$

Algebraic Identity Connecting Energetic Parameters

By evaluating the geometric vertical distances on both Figures 11.1 and 11.2, we derive the fundamental relationship connecting forward activation energy, reverse activation energy, and heat of reaction:

$$\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}}$$

Rearranging this relation yields the standard formula for calculating the reverse activation energy barrier:

$$E_{a,\text{r}} = E_{a,\text{f}} - \Delta H$$

Critical NESA Rule: Sign Conventions in Calculations

Activation energies ($E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$) are ALWAYS POSITIVE QUANTITIES ($E_a > 0$), as they represent an energy absorption required to climb from a baseline plateau to the transition state peak.

• For an exothermic reaction ($\Delta H < 0$), subtracting a negative number yields $E_{a,\text{r}} = E_{a,\text{f}} - (-\Delta H) = E_{a,\text{f}} + |\Delta H|$. Therefore, $E_{a,\text{r}} > E_{a,\text{f}}$.

• For an endothermic reaction ($\Delta H > 0$), subtracting a positive number yields $E_{a,\text{r}} = E_{a,\text{f}} - \Delta H$. Therefore, $E_{a,\text{f}} > E_{a,\text{r}}$.

3. The Transition State and Activated Complex

Sub-Microscopic Kinetics

When reactant particles collide with sufficient kinetic energy ($E \ge E_a$) and correct steric orientation, they do not instantaneously snap into product molecules. Instead, they pass through a fleeting, high-energy intermediate configuration termed the transition state ($\text{TS}^\ddagger$) or activated complex.

Structural Characteristics of $\text{TS}^\ddagger$

• Represents the maximum potential energy configuration along the reaction path.
• Involves partial bond breaking (reactant bonds stretching) and partial bond formation (product bonds forming) simultaneously.
• Extremely unstable with an infinitely short lifetime on the order of femtoseconds ($10^{-15}\text{ s}$).
• Cannot be isolated or stored as a chemical product.

Physical Meaning of Activation Energy

• $E_a$ is the mandatory kinetic energy input required during a collision to force electron clouds together, overcome electrostatic repulsion, and deform existing chemical bonds into the transition state geometry.
• Once the system reaches $\text{TS}^\ddagger$, it sits at the energy apex and can collapse forward into products or backward into reactants with equal thermodynamic ease!

4. Temperature Sensitivity and Differential Activation Energy Barriers

Kinetic Proof of LCP

Why does heating an equilibrium system shift it in the endothermic direction? While Le Chatelier's Principle provides a qualitative macroscopic rule ("absorb excess heat"), collision theory explains the sub-microscopic origin through the differential activation energy barriers ($E_{a,\text{f}}$ vs $E_{a,\text{r}}$).

Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution Analysis

At any given temperature $T$, molecular kinetic energies follow a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve. The area under the curve to the right of an activation energy line ($E \ge E_a$) represents the fraction ($f$) of total collisions possessing sufficient energy to react.

Figure 11.3: Maxwell-Boltzmann Kinetic Energy Distribution at $T_1$ and $T_2$ ($T_2 > T_1$) Superimposed with $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$ Thresholds

Qualitative Sensitivity Explanation: The Higher $E_a$ Rule

When temperature increases from $T_1$ to $T_2$:
• The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution flattens and shifts to the right, elevating the high-energy tail of the particle population.
• The absolute area under the curve increases for all energies beyond both activation energy thresholds ($E \ge E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E \ge E_{a,\text{r}}$).
• However, for the pathway possessing the HIGHER activation energy ($E_a$), the baseline fraction of active particles at lower temperatures is initially extremely small. Expanding the high-energy tail multiplies this small population by a much larger proportional (percentage) factor compared to the pathway with the lower activation energy.

Therefore, increasing temperature causes a larger percentage increase in the rate of the reaction direction with the higher activation energy!

5. Catalysts, Activation Energy, and Equilibrium Invariance

Catalytic Energetics

A catalyst accelerates a chemical reaction by providing an alternative reaction mechanism (e.g., surface adsorption, complexation) featuring a transition state of significantly lower potential energy.

Figure 11.4: Comparison of Uncatalysed (Solid Rose) and Catalysed (Dashed Emerald) Reaction Energy Profiles

Mathematical Proof: Why Catalysts Cannot Alter Enthalpy or Equilibrium

Let the uncatalysed forward and reverse activation energies be $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$, and let the catalyst lower the transition state energy peak by an amount $\Delta E_a$.

The new catalysed activation energy barriers become:

$$E_{a,\text{f}}' = E_{a,\text{f}} - \Delta E_a \quad \text{and} \quad E_{a,\text{r}}' = E_{a,\text{r}} - \Delta E_a$$

Calculating the new catalysed heat of reaction ($\Delta H'$):

$$\Delta H' = E_{a,\text{f}}' - E_{a,\text{r}}' = (E_{a,\text{f}} - \Delta E_a) - (E_{a,\text{r}} - \Delta E_a) = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}} = \Delta H$$

Summary of Catalytic Energetic Invariance

Because the catalyst reduces $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$ by the EXACT SAME ENERGY AMOUNT ($\Delta E_a$):

1. Heat of Reaction ($\Delta H$): Completely unchanged ($\Delta H' = \Delta H$).
2. Enthalpy Levels ($H_{\text{reactants}}, H_{\text{products}}$): Completely unchanged.
3. Forward and Reverse Rates ($r_{\mathrm{f}}, r_{\mathrm{r}}$): Accelerated by the exact same proportional factor, preserving $r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$ at all times.
4. Equilibrium Position & Yield: Completely unchanged (no shift occurs).

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Reverse Activation Energy Calculation

2 Marks

The synthesis of hydrogen iodide gas from its elements has a forward activation energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}$) of $+172\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ and an enthalpy change ($\Delta H$) of $+12\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$: $$\mathrm{H_{2(g)} + I_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2HI_{(g)}} \quad \Delta H = +12\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$ Calculate the reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$) for the decomposition of $\mathrm{HI_{(g)}}$.

Model Solution:

1. State the formula: $$E_{a,\text{r}} = E_{a,\text{f}} - \Delta H$$

2. Substitute the given values: $$E_{a,\text{r}} = (+172\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) - (+12\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) = +160\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$

3. Conclusion: The reverse activation energy for the decomposition of $\mathrm{HI_{(g)}}$ is $+160\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Interpreting Catalysed Energy Profiles

3 Marks

A reversible reaction has $H_{\text{reactants}} = +50\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$, $H_{\text{products}} = +110\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$, and an uncatalysed transition state energy of $+220\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. A catalyst lowers the transition state energy by $40\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
Determine the catalysed forward activation energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}'$) and prove that the enthalpy change ($\Delta H$) remains unchanged.

Model Solution:

1. Calculate Uncatalysed vs Catalysed Transition State Energy:
• $H_{\text{TS,uncat}} = +220\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
• $H_{\text{TS,cat}} = +220 - 40 = +180\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$

2. Calculate Catalysed Forward Activation Energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}'$): $$E_{a,\text{f}}' = H_{\text{TS,cat}} - H_{\text{reactants}} = +180 - (+50) = +130\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$

3. Prove Enthalpy Invariance:
• $\Delta H_{\text{uncat}} = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}} = +110 - (+50) = +60\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
• Catalysed reverse activation energy $E_{a,\text{r}}' = +180 - (+110) = +70\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
• $\Delta H_{\text{cat}} = E_{a,\text{f}}' - E_{a,\text{r}}' = +130 - (+70) = +60\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
Thus, $\Delta H$ remains identical at $+60\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Kinetic Explanation of Temperature Shift in Endothermic Systems

4 Marks

Consider the endothermic equilibrium: $\mathrm{N_2O_{4(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NO_{2(g)}} \quad (\Delta H = +57\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})$.
Using energy profile relationships and Maxwell-Boltzmann kinetic energy distributions, explain why heating this mixture causes a net forward shift.

Model Solution:

1. Barrier Relationship: Because the forward reaction is endothermic ($\Delta H = +57\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), $E_{a,\text{f}} > E_{a,\text{r}}$. The forward activation energy barrier is higher than the reverse barrier.

2. Maxwell-Boltzmann Temperature Effect: Heating increases average kinetic energy and flattens/broadens the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve, increasing the fraction of particles with $E \ge E_a$ for both pathways.

3. Proportional Rate Response: A higher activation energy barrier corresponds to an initially smaller fraction of successful collisions at baseline. Expanding the high-energy tail increases the fraction of collisions with $E \ge E_{a,\text{f}}$ by a larger percentage factor than those with $E \ge E_{a,\text{r}}$.

4. Conclusion: The forward rate increases more than the reverse rate ($r_{\mathrm{f}} > r_{\mathrm{r}}$), forcing a net forward shift ($\to$) towards $\mathrm{NO_2}$ until reactant consumption restores rate re-equalisation.

Worked Example 4 (HSC Exam Style): Calorimetry, Stoichiometry & Activation Energy Synthesis

5 Marks

A chemistry student performs a calorimetry experiment to determine the heat of reaction for the catalytic decomposition of aqueous hydrogen peroxide: $$\mathrm{2H_2O_{2(aq)} \xrightarrow{MnO_{2(s)}} 2H_2O_{(l)} + O_{2(g)}}$$ A $50.0\text{ mL}$ sample of a $1.20\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ solution is added to a coffee-cup calorimeter containing a small quantity of solid $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ catalyst. The temperature of the mixture rises from $21.5^\circ\text{C}$ to $35.8^\circ\text{C}$. (Assume the density of the solution is $1.00\text{ g mL}^{-1}$ and specific heat capacity $c = 4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}$).
The uncatalysed reverse activation energy for synthesising $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ from $\mathrm{H_2O}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$ is $+294\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

a) Calculate the heat absorbed by the solution ($q$) and determine the standard enthalpy change ($\Delta H$) per mole of $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ decomposed in $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. (3 Marks)
b) Calculate the uncatalysed forward activation energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}$) for the reaction. (1 Mark)
c) Explain, with reference to Maxwell-Boltzmann kinetic energy distributions, why adding $\mathrm{MnO_2(s)}$ increases the decomposition rate without altering $\Delta H$. (1 Mark)

Model Solution:

a) Calorimetry & Enthalpy Calculation:
1. Mass of solution: $m = V \times d = 50.0\text{ mL} \times 1.00\text{ g mL}^{-1} = 50.0\text{ g}$.
2. Temperature change: $\Delta T = 35.8 - 21.5 = 14.3\text{ K}$.
3. Heat absorbed by solution: $$q = mc\Delta T = 50.0\text{ g} \times 4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1} \times 14.3\text{ K} = 2988.7\text{ J} = 2.989\text{ kJ}$$
4. Moles of $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ reacted: $$n = c \times V = 1.20\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 0.0500\text{ L} = 0.0600\text{ mol}$$
5. Enthalpy change per mole of $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ (Exothermic reaction, $\Delta H < 0$): $$\Delta H = -\frac{q}{n} = -\frac{2.989\text{ kJ}}{0.0600\text{ mol}} = -49.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$ (Or $-99.6\text{ kJ}$ per 2 moles as stoichiometric coefficient in balanced equation).

b) Forward Activation Energy Calculation: $$\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}}$$ $$-49.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} = E_{a,\text{f}} - (+294\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})$$ $$E_{a,\text{f}} = 294 + (-49.8) = +244.2\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$

c) Catalytic Role & Maxwell-Boltzmann Explanation: $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower transition state energy peak, reducing both $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$ by the exact same amount ($\Delta E_a$). On a Maxwell-Boltzmann curve, a lower $E_a$ threshold increases the area representing the fraction of collisions with $E \ge E_a$, accelerating the reaction. Because $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$ are reduced equally, $\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}}' - E_{a,\text{r}}'$ remains completely unchanged.

Question 1 (Easy): Forward Activation Energy Calculation

2 Marks

A reversible reaction has a reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}$) of $+145\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ and an overall heat of reaction ($\Delta H$) of $-65\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. Calculate the forward activation energy ($E_{a,\text{f}}$).

Hint: Use $\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}}$ and rearrange for $E_{a,\text{f}}$. Remember that $\Delta H$ is negative!

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
• Formula: $E_{a,\text{f}} = E_{a,\text{r}} + \Delta H$
• Substitution: $E_{a,\text{f}} = 145\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} + (-65\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) = +80\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
The forward activation energy is $+80\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.

Question 2 (Medium): Catalysts and Energetic Invariance

3 Marks

Explain, with reference to potential energy profiles and activation energy barriers, why adding a catalyst increases the rate of both forward and reverse reactions equally without altering the enthalpy change ($\Delta H$) of the reaction.

Hint: Focus on how the catalyst lowers the transition state energy peak and what happens to the mathematical difference $(E_{a,\text{f}}' - E_{a,\text{r}}')$.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower transition state energy peak, reducing both forward ($E_{a,\text{f}}$) and reverse ($E_{a,\text{r}}$) activation energy barriers by the exact same amount ($\Delta E_a$).
Because both energy barriers are lowered equally, the proportion of collisions with $E \ge E_a'$ increases by the exact same factor in both directions, accelerating $r_{\mathrm{f}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{r}}$ equally.
Furthermore, because $\Delta H' = E_{a,\text{f}}' - E_{a,\text{r}}' = (E_{a,\text{f}} - \Delta E_a) - (E_{a,\text{r}} - \Delta E_a) = \Delta H$, the overall enthalpy change remains completely unchanged.

  • 1 Mark: Explains that catalyst lowers transition state energy peak and reduces both $E_{a,\text{f}}$ and $E_{a,\text{r}}$ by $\Delta E_a$.
  • 1 Mark: Links equal barrier lowering to equal rate acceleration in forward and reverse directions.
  • 1 Mark: Proves algebraically or conceptually that $\Delta H = E_{a,\text{f}} - E_{a,\text{r}}$ remains unchanged.

Question 3 (Hard): Kinetic Analysis of Temperature Shift in Exothermic Reactions

4 Marks

Using collision theory, activation energy relationships ($E_{a,\text{f}}$ vs $E_{a,\text{r}}$), and Maxwell-Boltzmann energy distribution concepts, explain why cooling an exothermic equilibrium system causes a net forward shift.

Hint: For exothermic forward reactions, which barrier is higher? How does cooling affect higher vs lower $E_a$ barriers?

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
1. Activation Energy Hierarchy: For an exothermic reaction ($\Delta H < 0$), $H_{\text{products}} < H_{\text{reactants}}$, which means $E_{a,\text{r}} > E_{a,\text{f}}$ (the reverse activation energy barrier is higher than the forward barrier).
2. Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution: Cooling reduces the average kinetic energy of molecules, shifting the distribution curve left and reducing the population of molecules with $E \ge E_a$.
3. Differential Rate Reduction: Because $E_{a,\text{r}}$ is higher, cooling causes a larger percentage decrease in the fraction of molecules exceeding $E_{a,\text{r}}$ compared to $E_{a,\text{f}}$.
4. Shift Direction: Consequently, the reverse rate drops much more severely than the forward rate ($r_{\mathrm{f}} > r_{\mathrm{r}}$), resulting in a net forward shift ($\to$) towards products.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies $E_{a,\text{r}} > E_{a,\text{f}}$ for an exothermic forward reaction.
  • 1 Mark: Explains that cooling reduces molecular kinetic energy and shifts Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution left.
  • 1 Mark: States that cooling disproportionately reduces the rate of the higher $E_a$ pathway ($r_{\mathrm{r}}$ drops more than $r_{\mathrm{f}}$).
  • 1 Mark: Concludes $r_{\mathrm{f}} > r_{\mathrm{r}}$ forces a net forward shift ($\to$).

Question 4 (HSC Challenge): Industrial Energetics & Reaction Profile Synthesis

5 Marks

The Haber process synthesises ammonia via: $\mathrm{N_{2(g)} + 3H_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NH_{3(g)}} \quad (\Delta H = -92\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})$.
The uncatalysed forward activation energy is $+250\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. An iron catalyst lowers the forward activation energy to $+80\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
Calculate the catalysed reverse activation energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}'$), sketch an annotated potential energy diagram comparing both pathways, and justify why moderate temperatures (400–450°C) are used industrially despite thermodynamics favouring low temperatures.

Hint: Balance the thermodynamic equilibrium yield (favoured at low $T$) against the kinetic rate of reaction (favoured at high $T$).

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
1. Catalysed Reverse Activation Energy ($E_{a,\text{r}}'$): $$E_{a,\text{r}}' = E_{a,\text{f}}' - \Delta H = (+80) - (-92) = +172\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$
2. Diagram Features: Sketch must show reactants plateau above products plateau ($\Delta H = -92\text{ kJ}$), uncatalysed peak at $+250\text{ kJ}$ above reactants, and catalysed peak at $+80\text{ kJ}$ above reactants.
3. Industrial Compromise Justification:
• Thermodynamic View: Because the forward reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H = -92\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), low temperatures shift equilibrium forward ($\to$), maximising equilibrium yield of $\mathrm{NH_3}$.
• Kinetic View: At low temperatures, kinetic energy is low and collisions exceeding $E_a$ are rare, making the reaction impractically slow.
• Compromise: A moderate temperature (400–450°C) alongside an iron catalyst provides an economically viable compromise between an acceptable reaction rate and a reasonable yield.

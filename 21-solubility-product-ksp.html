1. The Saturated Solution State & Dynamic Heterogeneous Equilibrium

Fundamental Concepts

In earlier lessons, ionic dissolution was introduced as a physical process where solvent water molecules hydrate individual cations and anions, pulling them away from an ionic crystal lattice. However, when an excess of a sparingly soluble ionic salt (such as silver chloride, $\mathrm{AgCl}$, or lead(II) iodide, $\mathrm{PbI_2}$) is added to water, the solution rapidly reaches a state known as a saturated solution.

A saturated solution is defined as a solution containing the maximum possible concentration of dissolved solute in stable thermodynamic equilibrium with undissolved solid solute at a specified temperature.

Sub-Microscopic Dynamic Equilibrium in Saturated Solutions

Macro-scopically, a saturated solution containing excess solid precipitate appears completely static; the mass of undissolved solid and the concentration of dissolved ions remain completely unchanged over time.

However, at the sub-microscopic level, the system is in a state of continuous, vigorous dynamic equilibrium! Two opposing processes occur at identical rates:

  1. Forward Process (Dissolution): Surface ions in the undissolved solid lattice break their ionic bonds and enter the aqueous phase as hydrated ions: $$\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = k_{\mathrm{fwd}} \times (\text{Surface Area})$$
  2. Reverse Process (Crystallisation / Precipitation): Hydrated mobile ions in solution collide with the solid surface, re-integrating into the crystalline ionic lattice: $$\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{crystallisation}} = k_{\mathrm{rev}} \times [\text{Cation}]^a [\text{Anion}]^b$$

At saturation, $\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = \text{Rate}_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$. Ions continuously enter and leave the solid phase at equal speeds, keeping macroscopic concentrations perfectly constant!

2. Deriving the $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Expression from General $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$

Mathematical Foundation

Consider a general sparingly soluble ionic compound with the chemical formula $A_x B_y$, where $x$ and $y$ represent the stoichiometric coefficients of cation $A^{m+}$ and anion $B^{n-}$ respectively.

When solid $A_x B_{y(s)}$ is placed in pure water, it establishes the following heterogeneous solubility equilibrium in a saturated solution: $$A_x B_{y(s)} \rightleftharpoons x A^{m+}_{(aq)} + y B^{n-}_{(aq)}$$

Step 1: Write the Standard Equilibrium Constant ($K_{\mathrm{eq}}$) Expression

Applying the Law of Mass Action (covered in Lesson 12), the general equilibrium constant expression for this reversible system is: $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{[A^{m+}_{(aq)}]^x [B^{n-}_{(aq)}]^y}{[A_x B_{y(s)}]}$$

Step 2: Apply the Pure Solid Concentration Rule

Notice the term $[A_x B_{y(s)}]$ in the denominator. $[A_x B_{y(s)}]$ represents the molar concentration (density divided by molar mass) of the pure solid phase.

Why is the Concentration of a Pure Solid Constant?

The molar concentration of a pure solid is defined as the amount of substance in moles per unit volume of the solid itself ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$): $$[\text{solid}] = \frac{n}{V} = \frac{m / M}{m / \rho} = \frac{\rho}{M}$$ where $\rho$ is the density ($\text{g L}^{-1}$) and $M$ is the molar mass ($\text{g mol}^{-1}$) of the compound.

Since density and molar mass are intrinsic physical constants of a solid substance at a given temperature, the concentration of a pure solid $[A_x B_{y(s)}]$ is a fixed constant, regardless of how much solid precipitate is sitting at the bottom of the beaker!

Step 3: Combine Constants to Define $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$

Because $[A_x B_{y(s)}]$ is constant, we multiply both sides of the $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ equation by $[A_x B_{y(s)}]$ to group all constant terms on the left-hand side: $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} \times [A_x B_{y(s)}] = [A^{m+}_{(aq)}]^x [B^{n-}_{(aq)}]^y$$

The product of the two constants ($K_{\mathrm{eq}} \times [A_x B_{y(s)}]$) is defined as a new, specialized equilibrium constant called the Solubility Product Constant ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}$):

The General $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Mathematical Definition

$$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [A^{m+}_{(aq)}]^x [B^{n-}_{(aq)}]^y$$

where $[A^{m+}]$ and $[B^{n-}]$ represent the equilibrium molar concentrations ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$) of the dissolved constituent ions in a saturated solution, raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients $x$ and $y$.

Crucial NESA Exam Rules for $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Expressions

  • 1. No Denominator: Pure solid reactants are assigned an activity of 1 and are NEVER included in a $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression. $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is strictly a product of aqueous ion concentrations!
  • 2. Saturated Solutions Only: A $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression applies EXCLUSIVELY when the solution is saturated and in dynamic equilibrium with undissolved solid.
  • 3. Units of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$: In the NESA Stage 6 Chemistry syllabus, $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is treated as a dimensionless thermodynamic constant (or expressed without explicit units) because ion concentrations are formally divided by standard state concentration ($c^\circ = 1\text{ mol L}^{-1}$).

4. Physical & Thermodynamic Significance of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$

Thermodynamic Analysis

The numerical magnitude of the solubility product constant ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}$) serves as a direct quantitative measure of a compound's intrinsic solubility in water at a given temperature:

Large $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Value ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} > 10^{-2}$)

Indicates that the dissolution equilibrium lies relatively far to the **RIGHT ($\to$)**. The compound dissolves to a significant extent, yielding high equilibrium ion concentrations before saturation occurs.

Very Small $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Value ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} < 10^{-10}$)

Indicates that the dissolution equilibrium lies heavily to the **LEFT ($\leftarrow$)**. The compound is extremely insoluble in water, reaching saturation at minute trace concentrations of dissolved ions (e.g. $\mathrm{AgI}$ with $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 8.5 \times 10^{-17}$).

1. Temperature Dependence of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$

Like all chemical equilibrium constants ($K_{\mathrm{eq}}$), the value of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ for a given compound is **STRICTLY TEMPERATURE DEPENDENT**.

For the vast majority of sparingly soluble ionic salts, the forward dissolution process is endothermic ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{dissolution}} > 0$), as the lattice enthalpy required to break ionic bonds exceeds the hydration enthalpy released when ions bond with water: $$\mathrm{Salt_{(s)} + Heat \rightleftharpoons Cation^+_{(aq)} + Anion^-_{(aq)}} \quad (\Delta H > 0)$$

Applying Le Chatelier's Principle to Temperature Changes

• Increasing Temperature: Adding thermal energy shifts the endothermic dissolution equilibrium to the RIGHT ($\to$) according to Le Chatelier's Principle. As a result, equilibrium ion concentrations $[A^{m+}]$ and $[B^{n-}]$ increase, causing the numerical value of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ to INCREASE.

• Decreasing Temperature: Removing thermal energy shifts the equilibrium to the LEFT ($\leftarrow$), causing ions to crystallise out of solution and $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ to DECREASE.

• Exam Warning: Always state the temperature (typically $25^\circ\text{C}$ / $298\text{ K}$) when citing experimental $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ values!

2. Critical Exam Pitfall: Comparing $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Values Across Different Stoichiometry Classes

A very common conceptual trap in HSC Chemistry is directly comparing the $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ values of two different salts to determine which salt is more soluble.

The Golden Rule of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Comparison

You can ONLY directly compare $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ values to rank solubility if the salts have the EXACT SAME STOICHIOMETRIC RATIO (i.e. same ion ratio)!

  • Valid Direct Comparison ($1:1$ vs $1:1$):
    $\mathrm{AgCl}$ ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.77 \times 10^{-10}$) versus $\mathrm{AgI}$ ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 8.52 \times 10^{-17}$).
    Since both are $1:1$ salts, $\mathrm{AgCl}$ has a larger $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and is unambiguously more soluble than $\mathrm{AgI}$.
  • INVALID Direct Comparison ($1:1$ vs $1:2$):
    $\mathrm{AgCl}$ ($1:1$ salt, $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.77 \times 10^{-10}$) versus $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$ ($2:1$ salt, $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.12 \times 10^{-12}$).
    Looking strictly at $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$, $\mathrm{AgCl}$ appears to have a larger constant ($10^{-10} > 10^{-12}$). However, calculating their actual molar solubilities reveals that $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$ ($s = 6.5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}$) is actually MORE SOLUBLE than $\mathrm{AgCl}$ ($s = 1.33 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}$)!
    Why? Because the squared term in $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}]^2 [\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}] = 4s^3$ alters the mathematical scaling relationship between $s$ and $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$!

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Deriving $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ for Complex $2:3$ Stoichiometry

3 Marks

Solid bismuth(III) sulfide ($\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)}}$) dissolves in pure water to form a saturated solution.

(a) Formulate the balanced heterogeneous equilibrium equation for the dissolution of $\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)}}$. (1 Mark)
(b) Derive the $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression starting from the general equilibrium constant expression $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$, explicitly explaining why the solid phase is omitted. (2 Marks)

Model Solution:

(a) Balanced Equilibrium Equation: $$\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)} \rightleftharpoons 2Bi^{3+}_{(aq)} + 3S^{2-}_{(aq)}}$$

(b) Derivation from $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$:
1. Write the general $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ expression: $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{[\mathrm{Bi^{3+}}]^2 [\mathrm{S^{2-}}]^3}{[\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)}}]}$$ 2. Since $\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)}}$ is a pure solid phase, its chemical activity and molar concentration $[\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)}}]$ are constant at a given temperature.
3. Multiplying $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ by $[\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)}}]$ yields the solubility product constant $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$: $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{eq}} \times [\mathrm{Bi_2S_{3(s)}}] = [\mathrm{Bi^{3+}}]^2 [\mathrm{S^{2-}}]^3$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Calculating $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ from Equilibrium Ion Concentrations

4 Marks

An chemical analyst prepares a saturated solution of lead(II) iodide ($\mathrm{PbI_2}$) at $25^\circ\text{C}$ by shaking solid $\mathrm{PbI_2}$ in distilled water until equilibrium is established.

Spectrophotometric analysis of the clear supernatant solution reveals that the equilibrium concentration of iodide ions is $[\mathrm{I^-}] = 2.60 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$.

Calculate the experimental value of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ for $\mathrm{PbI_2}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$.

Model Solution:

1. Write the balanced dissociation equation: $$\mathrm{PbI_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons Pb^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2I^-_{(aq)}}$$

2. State the $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression: $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}][\mathrm{I^-}]^2$$

3. Determine $[\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}]$ using stoichiometry:
From the balanced equation, every $1\text{ mole}$ of $\mathrm{PbI_2}$ that dissolves produces $1\text{ mole}$ of $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ and $2\text{ moles}$ of $\mathrm{I^-}$.
Therefore, the concentration of $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ is exactly half the concentration of $\mathrm{I^-}$: $$[\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}] = \frac{1}{2} [\mathrm{I^-}] = \frac{2.60 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}}{2} = 1.30 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$

4. Substitute equilibrium concentrations into $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$: $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = (1.30 \times 10^{-3}) \times (2.60 \times 10^{-3})^2$$ $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = (1.30 \times 10^{-3}) \times (6.76 \times 10^{-6}) = \mathbf{8.79 \times 10^{-9}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Evaluating Temperature Shift & Le Chatelier's Principle

5 Marks

The dissolution of silver chromate in water is an endothermic process: $$\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_{4(s)} \rightleftharpoons 2Ag^+_{(aq)} + CrO_{4(aq)}^{2-}} \quad (\Delta H = +61.2\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})$$ At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.12 \times 10^{-12}$.

(a) Predict and justify the effect on the numerical value of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ when the temperature of the saturated solution is increased to $60^\circ\text{C}$. (3 Marks)
(b) Explain why a direct comparison between the $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ of $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$ ($1.12 \times 10^{-12}$) and $\mathrm{AgCl}$ ($1.77 \times 10^{-10}$) cannot be used to conclude which salt has greater molar solubility. (2 Marks)

Model Solution:

(a) Temperature Effect on $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$:
• Prediction: $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ will **INCREASE** at $60^\circ\text{C}$.
• Justification: Since $\Delta H > 0$, the forward dissolution reaction absorbs thermal energy (endothermic). According to Le Chatelier's Principle, increasing system temperature causes the equilibrium position to shift in the **FORWARD direction ($\to$)** to absorb added heat.
• As a result, the equilibrium concentrations of products $[\mathrm{Ag^+}]$ and $[\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}]$ both increase. Because $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}]^2 [\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}]$, an increase in product concentrations directly increases the numerical magnitude of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$.

(b) Explanation of Comparison Limitations:
• $\mathrm{AgCl}$ is a $1:1$ salt ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}][\mathrm{Cl^-}] = s^2$).
• $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$ is a $2:1$ salt ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}]^2 [\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}] = 4s^3$).
• Because the two salts have different stoichiometric ratios, their $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expressions have different mathematical forms and powers. Consequently, $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ values scale differently with respect to molar solubility ($s$), making a direct raw comparison of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ values mathematically invalid.

Question 4 (Extension): Evaluating Thermodynamic Equilibrium & Dissolution Dynamics

5 Marks

A student places $5.0\text{ g}$ of solid silver chloride ($\mathrm{AgCl}$) into $1.0\text{ L}$ of distilled water in a sealed, insulated flask at $25^\circ\text{C}$ and stirs vigorously.

(a) Explain why the mass of undissolved $\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)}}$ eventually stops decreasing, even though dynamic ionic exchanges continue occurring at the crystal surface. (3 Marks)
(b) Describe what would happen to the value of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ if an additional $10.0\text{ g}$ of solid $\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)}}$ were added to the saturated mixture at constant temperature. Justify your answer. (2 Marks)

Hint: Focus on dynamic equilibrium rates ($\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = \text{Rate}_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$) and the intensive nature of equilibrium constants.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
(a) Explanation of Dynamic Equilibrium:
• Initially, solid $\mathrm{AgCl}$ dissolves rapidly ($\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{dissolution}} > \text{Rate}_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$), causing $[\mathrm{Ag^+}]$ and $[\mathrm{Cl^-}]$ to rise.
• As aqueous ion concentrations increase, the rate of ion collisions with the solid surface increases, accelerating the reverse rate of crystallisation.
• When the solution becomes saturated, the rate of crystallisation equals the rate of dissolution ($\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{crystallisation}} = \text{Rate}_{\mathrm{dissolution}}$).
• At this dynamic equilibrium point, the macroscopic mass of solid undissolved $\mathrm{AgCl}$ remains constant, even though individual ions continually dissolve and recrystallise at equal rates.

(b) Effect of Adding Excess Solid:
• Effect: The numerical value of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ will **REMAIN ENTIRELY UNCHANGED**.
• Justification: $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is an intensive thermodynamic equilibrium constant that depends strictly on temperature. Adding more pure solid $\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)}}$ does not change the activity of the solid phase (activity = 1) nor does it change the equilibrium ion concentrations in an already saturated solution. Thus, $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ remains constant.

  • 1 Mark: Identifies saturation condition where $\text{Rate}_{\mathrm{dissolution}} = \text{Rate}_{\mathrm{crystallisation}}$.
  • 1 Mark: Clarifies that macroscopic constancy masks microscopic dynamic exchange.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly states that $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ remains unchanged upon adding extra solid.
  • 1 Mark: Justifies that $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ depends strictly on temperature and solid activity remains 1.

1. Foundations of Chemical Reversibility

Theory

In introductory chemistry, chemical transformations are frequently modelled as standard unidirectional processes where reactants convert fully into products (reactions going to completion). However, many chemical and physical processes are reversible, meaning products can collide and reform the original reactants under suitable conditions.

Reversible Reactions ($\rightleftharpoons$)

A reaction is reversible when the activation energy for the reverse pathway ($E_{a(\text{reverse})}$) is sufficiently low that colliding product particles possess enough kinetic energy to break bonds and reform reactants.

  • Occur in closed systems where matter cannot escape.
  • Do not go to completion; an equilibrium mixture of reactants and products is established.
  • Represented using equilibrium arrows ($\rightleftharpoons$).

Irreversible Reactions ($\rightarrow$)

A reaction is irreversible when products cannot reform reactants under standard conditions. This occurs due to an extremely high reverse activation energy ($E_{a(\text{reverse})}$) or the removal of products from the reaction sphere.

  • Driven by a large decrease in enthalpy ($\Delta H \ll 0$) and/or large positive entropy change ($\Delta S > 0$).
  • Goes to completion until the limiting reagent is entirely consumed.
  • Represented using a unidirectional arrow ($\rightarrow$).
Thermodynamic Driver (Gibbs Free Energy): $$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$$ For a reaction to be reversible under laboratory conditions, $\Delta G$ for both the forward and reverse pathways must be accessible at reasonable temperatures, preventing the system from locking permanently at completion.

At the microscopic level, collision theory dictates that for any chemical collision to result in a chemical transformation, the colliding species must possess energy equal to or exceeding the activation energy barrier ($E \ge E_a$) and collide in a favorable steric orientation. In a reversible reaction, both the forward collision pathway (reactants to products) and the reverse collision pathway (products to reactants) have accessible activation energy barriers. Consequently, as products accumulate, reverse collisions occur at an appreciable rate alongside forward collisions.

3. Detailed Practical Analysis of NESA Systems

Syllabus Practicals

System A: Cobalt(II) Chloride Hydration System

Dissolving anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride ($\mathrm{CoCl_2}$) in water establishes an endothermic equilibrium between the pink hexaaquacobalt(II) complex and the blue tetrachlorocobaltate(II) complex:

$$\mathrm{\underbrace{Co(H_2O)_6^{2+}(aq)}_{\text{Pink (Hydrated)}} + 4Cl^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons \underbrace{CoCl_4^{2-}(aq)}_{\text{Blue (Anhydrous Form)}} + 6H_2O(l)} \quad \Delta H > 0$$

Observations & Reversibility Criteria: Adding concentrated $\mathrm{HCl}$ increases $[\mathrm{Cl^-}]$, driving the reaction forward to turn blue. Adding $\mathrm{H_2O}$ dilutes $[\mathrm{Cl^-}]$, driving the reverse reaction to restore the pink color. Because both forward and reverse processes readily proceed by altering concentration or temperature without permanent loss of components in a closed vessel, the reaction is fully reversible.

System B: Iron(III) Nitrate and Potassium Thiocyanate System

Mixing pale yellow iron(III) nitrate ($\mathrm{Fe(NO_3)_3}$) with colourless potassium thiocyanate ($\mathrm{KSCN}$) produces a deep blood-red iron(III) thiocyanate complex ion:

$$\mathrm{\underbrace{Fe^{3+}(aq)}_{\text{Pale Yellow}} + \underbrace{SCN^-(aq)}_{\text{Colourless}} \rightleftharpoons \underbrace{FeSCN^{2+}(aq)}_{\text{Blood-Red}}} \quad \Delta H < 0$$

Observations & Reversibility Criteria:

  • Adding $\mathrm{Fe(NO_3)_3}$ or $\mathrm{KSCN}$ increases reactant concentration, shifting equilibrium forward and intensifying the red colour.
  • Adding $\mathrm{AgNO_3}$ precipitates $\mathrm{AgSCN(s)}$ ($\mathrm{Ag^+(aq) + SCN^-(aq) \rightarrow AgSCN(s)}$), removing $\mathrm{SCN^-}$ ions from solution. This drives the reverse reaction, causing the blood-red color to fade to pale yellow.
  • The system's capacity to shift repeatedly in response to concentration adjustments demonstrates dynamic reversibility.

System C: Combustion of Magnesium Ribbon

Burning magnesium metal in oxygen generates an intense white light and produces magnesium oxide powder:

$$\mathrm{2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2MgO(s)} \quad \Delta H^\circ = -1206\text{ kJ/mol}$$

Observations & Irreversibility Criteria:

  • Thermodynamic Barrier: The forward reaction releases a vast amount of heat energy ($\Delta H^\circ = -1206\text{ kJ/mol}$). Reversing this reaction requires supplying $+1206\text{ kJ/mol}$ of activation energy, making the reverse pathway impossible under normal laboratory conditions.
  • Entropy & Open System Loss: Energy and light radiate into the surroundings, and gas particles escape in an open container, preventing recombining collisions. Therefore, combustion is strictly irreversible.

4. Graded HSC Worked Examples

Model Answers
Worked Example 1: Identifying Reversibility Easy • 2 Marks

Question: State whether the dissolving of blue cobalt(II) chloride paper in water to turn pink is a reversible or irreversible process, and justify your answer with reference to microscopic changes.

Step 1: State the classification

The hydration of cobalt(II) chloride paper is a reversible process.

Step 2: Provide microscopic justification using chemical species

When water is added to blue anhydrous $\mathrm{CoCl_4^{2-}}$ paper, water molecules coordinate around the cobalt centre to form pink $\mathrm{[Co(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ ions. Upon heating the pink paper, water evaporates, forcing chloride ions back into coordination to reform blue $\mathrm{CoCl_4^{2-}}$. Because the activation energy for both forward and reverse hydration pathways is low, particles can readily reform both reactants and products.

Worked Example 2: Analyzing Precipitate Stress on Equilibrium Medium • 3 Marks

Question: A student adds a few drops of $0.1\text{ M }\mathrm{AgNO_3}$ to an equilibrium mixture containing $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq)}$ (pale yellow), $\mathrm{SCN^-(aq)}$ (colourless), and $\mathrm{FeSCN^{2+}(aq)}$ (blood-red). Describe the expected colour change and account for this observation using collision theory.

Step 1: Identify chemical side-reaction

Addition of $\mathrm{AgNO_3}$ introduces $\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)}$ ions, which react with $\mathrm{SCN^-(aq)}$ to form an insoluble white precipitate of silver thiocyanate: $\mathrm{Ag^+(aq) + SCN^-(aq) \rightarrow AgSCN(s)}$.

Step 2: Relate to collision theory and directional shift

Precipitation dramatically decreases the concentration of free $\mathrm{SCN^-(aq)}$ ions in solution. As a result, the frequency of successful collisions between $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ and $\mathrm{SCN^-}$ decreases relative to the rate of decomposition of $\mathrm{FeSCN^{2+}}$. The reverse reaction temporarily exceeds the forward reaction rate ($\text{rate}_{\text{reverse}} > \text{rate}_{\text{forward}}$).

Step 3: State macroscopic observation

The concentration of blood-red $\mathrm{FeSCN^{2+}}$ decreases while pale yellow $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ increases, causing the solution to lighten from deep blood-red to a pale yellow-orange colour.

Worked Example 3: Thermodynamic Analysis of Magnesium Combustion Hard • 4 Marks

Question: Account for the irreversibility of magnesium ribbon combustion ($\mathrm{2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2MgO(s)}$) using an enthalpy-level diagram showing all critical information, enthalpy change ($\Delta H$), and entropy ($\Delta S$) considerations.

Step 1: Enthalpy & Activation Energy Analysis

The combustion of magnesium is extremely exothermic ($\Delta H^\circ = -1206\text{ kJ/mol}$). As shown in the enthalpy-level profile above, the products ($\mathrm{2MgO}$) sit in a deep enthalpy well far below the reactants ($\mathrm{2Mg + O_2}$). Consequently, the reverse activation energy ($E_{a(\text{reverse})} = E_{a(\text{forward})} + |\Delta H| = 144 + 1206 = 1350\text{ kJ/mol}$) is exceptionally large. At standard laboratory temperatures, colliding $\mathrm{MgO}$ particles possess insufficient kinetic energy to overcome this immense $E_{a(\text{r})}$ barrier.

Step 2: Entropy Analysis

The reaction consumes gaseous oxygen to form solid magnesium oxide ($\mathrm{1\text{ mol gas} \rightarrow 0\text{ mol gas}}$), representing a negative system entropy change ($\Delta S_{\text{system}} < 0$). However, the vast amount of thermal energy released into the surroundings creates a massive positive entropy shift in the surroundings ($\Delta S_{\text{surroundings}} = -\frac{\Delta H}{T} \gg 0$), ensuring that $\Delta S_{\text{total}} \gg 0$.

Step 3: Conclusion on Gibbs Free Energy

Because $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S \ll 0$, the forward reaction is overwhelmingly spontaneous, whereas the reverse reaction has a huge positive $\Delta G$, rendering the process strictly irreversible under standard laboratory conditions.

Worked Example 4: Evaluating Microscopic Reversibility Using Collision Theory Hard • 4 Marks

Question: Explain how collision theory accounts for the difference between a reversible chemical reaction that establishes dynamic equilibrium and an irreversible reaction that goes to completion, referencing energy requirements and activation energy barriers.

Step 1: Define collision requirements for reversible systems

For a chemical reaction to be reversible, colliding product particles must be capable of reforming reactant particles. According to collision theory, this requires that the reverse activation energy ($E_{a(\text{reverse})}$) is sufficiently low that a significant fraction of colliding product particles possess kinetic energy $E \ge E_{a(\text{reverse})}$ and collide with correct molecular orientation.

Step 2: Explain dynamic equilibrium formation

As reactant particles convert into product particles, the concentration of products increases, raising the collision frequency between product molecules. Eventually, the rate of successful reverse collisions increases until it equals the rate of successful forward collisions ($\text{rate}_{\text{forward}} = \text{rate}_{\text{reverse}} > 0$), establishing dynamic equilibrium in a closed system.

Step 3: Contrast with irreversible systems

In an irreversible reaction (such as combustion), the forward reaction is typically highly exothermic ($\Delta H \ll 0$), placing the reverse activation energy ($E_{a(\text{reverse})} = E_{a(\text{forward})} + |\Delta H|$) extremely high. At ambient conditions, virtually zero product particles possess kinetic energy $E \ge E_{a(\text{reverse})}$. As a result, reverse collisions are ineffective, and the reaction continues in the forward direction until the limiting reactant is completely consumed.

Question 3: Contrasting Open vs Closed Systems in Reversibility

4 Marks

Contrast the physical reversibility of heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate crystals ($\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O}$) in an open crucible versus heating them in a sealed stoppered test tube.

Hint: Think about whether water vapour ($\mathrm{H_2O(g)}$) can leave the system boundary and how this impacts recombining collisions.

Model Answer & Rubric

Equation: $\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O(s) \rightleftharpoons CuSO_4(s) + 5H_2O(g)}$

Open Crucible (Irreversible Behaviour): In an open crucible, thermal energy converts structural water into gaseous water vapor ($\mathrm{H_2O(g)}$), which escapes into the surrounding atmosphere. Because product particles leave the reaction container, reverse collisions between anhydrous $\mathrm{CuSO_4(s)}$ and $\mathrm{H_2O(g)}$ cannot occur. The reaction goes to completion to leave white anhydrous powder.

Sealed Test Tube (Reversible Equilibrium): In a sealed tube (closed system), water vapour is trapped inside. As gaseous $\mathrm{H_2O}$ accumulates, the reverse reaction rate increases until $\text{rate}_{\text{forward}} = \text{rate}_{\text{reverse}}$, establishing dynamic equilibrium where both blue hydrated solid and white anhydrous solid coexist.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies open system allows gas to escape while closed system retains gaseous products.
  • 1 Mark: Explains open crucible behavior using collision theory (loss of reactant collisions).
  • 1 Mark: Explains sealed tube behavior establishing dynamic equilibrium.
  • 1 Mark: Relates observations to colour changes (blue $\leftrightarrow$ white).

Question 4: Activation Energy Profiles and Reversibility Analysis

4 Marks

A student investigates a hypothetical reaction $\mathrm{A_2(g) + B_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2AB(g)}$ where $E_{a(\text{forward})} = 180\text{ kJ/mol}$ and $\Delta H = -95\text{ kJ/mol}$. Calculate the reverse activation energy $E_{a(\text{reverse})}$ and evaluate, using collision theory and activation energy considerations, whether this reaction is more likely to be reversible or go to completion under standard conditions.

Hint: Use the relationship $\Delta H = E_{a(\text{forward})} - E_{a(\text{reverse})}$. Compare $E_{a(\text{reverse})}$ with $E_{a(\text{forward})}$ to assess whether product particles can easily overcome the reverse barrier.

Model Answer & Rubric

Calculation:

$$\Delta H = E_{a(\text{forward})} - E_{a(\text{reverse})}$$ $$-95\text{ kJ/mol} = 180\text{ kJ/mol} - E_{a(\text{reverse})}$$ $$E_{a(\text{reverse})} = 180 + 95 = 275\text{ kJ/mol}$$

Reversibility Analysis: The reverse activation energy barrier ($275\text{ kJ/mol}$) is significantly higher than the forward barrier ($180\text{ kJ/mol}$). Under standard room temperature conditions, colliding product molecules ($\mathrm{AB}$) rarely possess kinetic energy $E \ge 275\text{ kJ/mol}$. Consequently, reverse collisions occur at an extremely low rate compared to forward collisions, causing the reaction to behave as if it goes to completion (effectively irreversible) unless energy is supplied to increase molecular kinetic energy.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates $E_{a(\text{reverse})} = 275\text{ kJ/mol}$ with correct working and units.
  • 1 Mark: Compares forward and reverse activation energies.
  • 1 Mark: Applies collision theory to state that few product particles have $E \ge 275\text{ kJ/mol}$ at room temperature.
  • 1 Mark: Concludes that the high reverse activation energy causes the reaction to go to completion / behave irreversibly under standard conditions.

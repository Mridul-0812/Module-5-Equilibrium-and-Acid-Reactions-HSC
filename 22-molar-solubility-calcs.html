1. Fundamental Definitions & Unit Conversion Architecture

Unit Foundations

In quantitative chemical analysis, the solubility of a solute can be expressed using several distinct physical units depending on whether the context is industrial, clinical, or analytical. Distinguishing between thermodynamic constants ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}$) and measured physical solubilities ($s$ or $S_{\text{mass}}$) is essential.

1. Molar Solubility ($s$)

• Definition: The number of moles of a solid solute that dissolve per litre of saturated solution at dynamic equilibrium at a specified temperature.
• Symbol: $s$
• SI Units: $\text{mol L}^{-1}$ (or $\text{M}$)
• Role: $s$ is the primary variable used in ICE tables and $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ algebraic expressions!

2. Mass Solubility ($S_{\text{mass}}$)

• Definition: The mass of solid solute in grams that dissolves per litre of saturated solution at dynamic equilibrium.
• Symbol: $S_{\text{mass}}$
• Common Units: $\text{g L}^{-1}$, $\text{mg L}^{-1}$, or $\text{g per 100 mL}$
• Role: $S_{\text{mass}}$ directly connects laboratory gravimetric mass measurements to chemical mole quantities.

The Universal Unit Conversion Matrix

Converting between mass solubility ($S_{\text{mass}}$), molar solubility ($s$), and the solubility product constant ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}$) follows a strict linear conversion path mediated by the compound's molar mass ($M$, in $\text{g mol}^{-1}$) and stoichiometric ratio factors:

Key Unit Conversion Formulae

$$\text{Molar Solubility } s \; (\text{mol L}^{-1}) = \frac{\text{Mass Solubility } S_{\text{mass}} \; (\text{g L}^{-1})}{\text{Molar Mass } M \; (\text{g mol}^{-1})}$$ $$\text{Mass Solubility } S_{\text{mass}} \; (\text{g L}^{-1}) = s \; (\text{mol L}^{-1}) \times M \; (\text{g mol}^{-1})$$ $$\text{Concentration } S_{\text{mg/L}} \; (\text{mg L}^{-1}) = S_{\text{mass}} \; (\text{g L}^{-1}) \times 1000 = s \times M \times 1000$$ $$\text{Mass per 100 mL } S_{100\text{mL}} \; (\text{g / 100 mL}) = \frac{S_{\text{mass}} \; (\text{g L}^{-1})}{10}$$

2. Deriving $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = f(s)$ Equations for All Salt Stoichiometries

Algebraic Derivations

When a solid ionic compound dissolves in pure water, the concentration of each dissolved ion at equilibrium is directly proportional to the molar solubility $s$ of the salt. The constant of proportionality is given by the stoichiometric coefficient of that ion in the balanced dissociation equation.

To eliminate confusion and master NESA calculations, we derive the mathematical formula relating $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and $s$ for every major salt stoichiometry class step-by-step:

1. $1:1$ Salt Stoichiometry ($AB$)

Examples: $\mathrm{AgCl}, \mathrm{BaSO_4}, \mathrm{CaCO_3}$

$$\mathrm{AB_{(s)} \rightleftharpoons A^+_{(aq)} + B^-_{(aq)}}$$

• If $s\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ of solid $\mathrm{AB}$ dissolves:
  $[\mathrm{A^+}] = s \quad \text{and} \quad [\mathrm{B^-}] = s$

$$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{A^+}][\mathrm{B^-}] = (s)(s) = \mathbf{s^2}$$
Reverse Solving for $s$: $$s = \sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}$$

2. $1:2$ or $2:1$ Salt Stoichiometry ($AB_2$ / $A_2B$)

Examples: $\mathrm{PbCl_2}, \mathrm{CaF_2}, \mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$

$$\mathrm{AB_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons A^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2B^-_{(aq)}}$$

• If $s\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ of solid $\mathrm{AB_2}$ dissolves:
  $[\mathrm{A^{2+}}] = s \quad \text{and} \quad [\mathrm{B^-}] = \mathbf{2s}$

$$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{A^{2+}}][\mathrm{B^-}]^2 = (s)(2s)^2 = (s)(4s^2) = \mathbf{4s^3}$$
Reverse Solving for $s$: $$s = \sqrt[3]{\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}{4}}$$

3. $1:3$ or $3:1$ Salt Stoichiometry ($AB_3$ / $A_3B$)

Examples: $\mathrm{Fe(OH)_3}, \mathrm{Al(OH)_3}, \mathrm{Ag_3PO_4}$

$$\mathrm{AB_{3(s)} \rightleftharpoons A^{3+}_{(aq)} + 3B^-_{(aq)}}$$

• If $s\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ of solid $\mathrm{AB_3}$ dissolves:
  $[\mathrm{A^{3+}}] = s \quad \text{and} \quad [\mathrm{B^-}] = \mathbf{3s}$

$$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{A^{3+}}][\mathrm{B^-}]^3 = (s)(3s)^3 = (s)(27s^3) = \mathbf{27s^4}$$
Reverse Solving for $s$: $$s = \sqrt[4]{\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}{27}}$$

4. $2:3$ or $3:2$ Salt Stoichiometry ($A_3B_2$ / $A_2B_3$)

Examples: $\mathrm{Ca_3(PO_4)_2}, \mathrm{Bi_2S_3}$

$$\mathrm{A_3B_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons 3A^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2B^{3-}_{(aq)}}$$

• If $s\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ of solid dissolves:
  $[\mathrm{A^{2+}}] = \mathbf{3s} \quad \text{and} \quad [\mathrm{B^{3-}}] = \mathbf{2s}$

$$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{A^{2+}}]^3 [\mathrm{B^{3-}}]^2 = (3s)^3 (2s)^2 = (27s^3)(4s^2) = \mathbf{108s^5}$$
Reverse Solving for $s$: $$s = \sqrt[5]{\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}{108}}$$

CRITICAL EXAM WARNING: The "Double-Counting" Misconception!

The most common mistake made by HSC Chemistry students in Module 5 calculations occurs when evaluating $1:2$ salts like $\mathrm{PbCl_2}$.

• THE ERROR: Students confuse the concentration multiplier ($2s$) with the exponent power ($^2$). Some omit the factor of $2$ inside the bracket, writing $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = (s)(s)^2 = s^3$ (INCORRECT!). Others double-count by doubling an already measured $[\mathrm{Cl^-}]$ concentration value!

• THE CORRECT RULE:
1. If you know molar solubility $s$, $[\mathrm{Cl^-}] = 2s$. You MUST both multiply by 2 AND square the term: $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = (s)(2s)^2 = 4s^3$.
2. If an exam question provides the actual measured equilibrium concentration of chloride ions $[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_{\mathrm{eq}}$, that number ALREADY incorporates the factor of 2! Do not multiply it by 2 again before squaring!

3. The ICE Table Protocol for Solution Dissolution

Systematic Algorithm

For complex multi-step problems or when transitioning between pure water and modified solutions, constructing a formal **ICE (Initial, Change, Equilibrium)** table provides an error-free mathematical scaffold.

Setting Up an ICE Table in Pure Water

Suppose solid lead(II) iodide ($\mathrm{PbI_2}$) is added to pure distilled water until saturation is reached:

Reaction State $\mathrm{PbI_{2(s)}}$ $\rightleftharpoons$ $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}_{(aq)}}$ $+$ $2\mathrm{I^-_{(aq)}}$
Initial Conc. ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$) Pure Solid Phase — $0.000$ — $0.000$
Change ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$) -s dissolves — $+s$ — $+2s$
Equilibrium Conc. ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$) Excess Solid Remaining — $\mathbf{s}$ — $\mathbf{2s}$

Master Step-by-Step Problem Solving Algorithm

  1. Step 1: Write the Balanced Dissociation Equation. Always include state symbols ($\text{s}$ for solid precipitate, $\text{aq}$ for aqueous ions).
  2. Step 2: Construct the ICE Table. Express equilibrium concentrations of all aqueous ions in terms of the single unknown $s$.
  3. Step 3: Substitute into the $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ Expression. Write $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\text{Cation}]^a [\text{Anion}]^b$ and substitute the algebraic terms from the 'E' row.
  4. Step 4: Solve for $s$ algebraically. Perform root extractions ($\sqrt{}$, $\sqrt[3]{}$, $\sqrt[4]{}$, or $\sqrt[5]{}$).
  5. Step 5: Convert Units & Apply Significant Figures. Convert $s$ ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$) to mass solubility ($\text{g L}^{-1}$) if required by multiplying by molar mass $M$. Round the final answer to match the lowest number of significant figures provided in the prompt.

4. Volume-Limited Mass Calculations & Real-World Applications

Practical Stoichiometry

A critical practical application of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ in NESA Module 5 is calculating the maximum mass ($m_{\text{max}}$, in grams) of a sparingly soluble salt that can dissolve in a specific volume ($V$, in litres) of water before precipitation begins.

Maximum Dissolved Mass Formula

$$m_{\text{max}} = n_{\text{max}} \times M = (s \times V) \times M$$

where $s$ is molar solubility ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$), $V$ is water volume ($\text{L}$), and $M$ is compound molar mass ($\text{g mol}^{-1}$).

Real-World Medical & Clinical Context: Barium Meal Safety

In medical diagnostic radiology, patients undergoing X-ray imaging of the gastrointestinal tract swallow a thick suspension of barium sulfate ($\mathrm{BaSO_4}$), known as a "barium meal".

Toxicity of Soluble Barium Ions ($\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$)

Free aqueous $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ ions are highly toxic to humans, causing muscle paralysis, cardiac arrhythmias, and kidney failure. Soluble barium salts such as barium chloride ($\mathrm{BaCl_2}$) are lethal poisons even at milligram doses!

Safety of Insoluble Barium Sulfate ($\mathrm{BaSO_4}$)

$\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ has an extremely small $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.08 \times 10^{-10}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$.
Calculating its molar solubility: $$s = \sqrt{1.08 \times 10^{-10}} = 1.04 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$ In a $250\text{ mL}$ medical dose, the total mass of dissolved toxic $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ is only: $$m = 1.04 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M} \times 0.250\text{ L} \times 137.33\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{0.00035\text{ g}} \quad (0.35\text{ mg!})$$ This tiny quantity is completely harmless, allowing $\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ to pass safely through the digestive tract without being absorbed into the bloodstream!

Worked Example 1 (Easy): $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ from Molar Solubility ($1:1$ Salt)

2 Marks

The molar solubility of silver chloride ($\mathrm{AgCl}$) in pure water at $25^\circ\text{C}$ is $1.33 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$.
Calculate the solubility product constant ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}$) of $\mathrm{AgCl}$ at this temperature.

Model Solution:

1. Write the balanced dissociation equation: $$\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)} \rightleftharpoons Ag^+_{(aq)} + Cl^-_{(aq)}}$$

2. Express equilibrium concentrations in terms of $s$: $$[\mathrm{Ag^+}] = s = 1.33 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$ $$[\mathrm{Cl^-}] = s = 1.33 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$

3. Substitute into $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression: $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}][\mathrm{Cl^-}] = s^2$$ $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = (1.33 \times 10^{-5})^2 = \mathbf{1.77 \times 10^{-10}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Molar and Mass Solubility from $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ ($1:2$ Salt)

3 Marks

The solubility product constant of lead(II) fluoride ($\mathrm{PbF_2}$) at $25^\circ\text{C}$ is $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 3.30 \times 10^{-8}$.

(a) Calculate the molar solubility ($s$) of $\mathrm{PbF_2}$ in pure water at $25^\circ\text{C}$. (2 Marks)
(b) Calculate the mass solubility of $\mathrm{PbF_2}$ in $\text{g L}^{-1}$. ($M(\mathrm{PbF_2}) = 245.2\text{ g mol}^{-1}$) (1 Mark)

Model Solution:

(a) Molar Solubility ($s$):
1. Write dissociation equation: $\mathrm{PbF_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons Pb^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2F^-_{(aq)}}$
2. Formulate $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ in terms of $s$: $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}][\mathrm{F^-}]^2 = (s)(2s)^2 = 4s^3$$ 3. Rearrange and solve for $s$: $$s^3 = \frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}{4} = \frac{3.30 \times 10^{-8}}{4} = 8.25 \times 10^{-9}$$ $$s = \sqrt[3]{8.25 \times 10^{-9}} = \mathbf{2.02 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

(b) Mass Solubility ($S_{\text{mass}}$): $$S_{\text{mass}} = s \times M = 2.02 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 245.2\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{0.495\text{ g L}^{-1}}$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Multi-Unit Conversion from Gravimetric Data

4 Marks

A student dissolves excess silver chromate ($\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$) in $250.0\text{ mL}$ of distilled water at $25^\circ\text{C}$. After filtering off the undissolved precipitate and evaporating the clear filtrate to dryness, $0.00538\text{ g}$ of solid $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$ is recovered.

Calculate the experimental value of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ for $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$. ($M(\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}) = 331.73\text{ g mol}^{-1}$)

Model Solution:

1. Calculate moles of dissolved $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}$: $$n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{0.00538\text{ g}}{331.73\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 1.6218 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol}$$

2. Calculate molar solubility $s$ ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$): $$V = 250.0\text{ mL} = 0.2500\text{ L}$$ $$s = \frac{n}{V} = \frac{1.6218 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol}}{0.2500\text{ L}} = 6.487 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$

3. Write balanced equation and $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ expression: $$\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_{4(s)} \rightleftharpoons 2Ag^+_{(aq)} + CrO_{4(aq)}^{2-}}$$ $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}]^2 [\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}] = (2s)^2 (s) = 4s^3$$

4. Substitute $s$ into $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ formula: $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 4 \times (6.487 \times 10^{-5})^3$$ $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 4 \times (2.7299 \times 10^{-13}) = \mathbf{1.09 \times 10^{-12}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension): $2:3$ Salt Derivation & Volume Requirement

5 Marks

The solubility product constant of calcium phosphate ($\mathrm{Ca_3(PO_4)_2}$) at $25^\circ\text{C}$ is $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 2.07 \times 10^{-33}$. ($M(\mathrm{Ca_3(PO_4)_2}) = 310.18\text{ g mol}^{-1}$)

(a) Derive the algebraic relationship between $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and $s$ for $\mathrm{Ca_3(PO_4)_2}$. (2 Marks)
(b) Calculate the minimum volume of pure water in litres required to completely dissolve a $1.00\text{ g}$ sample of $\mathrm{Ca_3(PO_4)_2}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$. (3 Marks)

Model Solution:

(a) Algebraic Derivation: $$\mathrm{Ca_3(PO_4)_{2(s)} \rightleftharpoons 3Ca^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2PO_{4(aq)}^{3-}}$$ $$[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}] = 3s \quad \text{and} \quad [\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}] = 2s$$ $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]^3 [\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}]^2 = (3s)^3 (2s)^2 = (27s^3)(4s^2) = \mathbf{108s^5}$$

(b) Calculate Minimum Water Volume $V$:
1. Solve for molar solubility $s$: $$s^5 = \frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}{108} = \frac{2.07 \times 10^{-33}}{108} = 1.9167 \times 10^{-35}$$ $$s = \sqrt[5]{1.9167 \times 10^{-35}} = 1.1387 \times 10^{-7}\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$
2. Calculate moles in $1.00\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{Ca_3(PO_4)_2}$: $$n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{1.00\text{ g}}{310.18\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 3.2239 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$$
3. Calculate required volume $V = \frac{n}{s}$: $$V = \frac{3.2239 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}}{1.1387 \times 10^{-7}\text{ mol L}^{-1}} = \mathbf{2.83 \times 10^4\text{ L}} \quad (28,300\text{ Litres!})$$

5 Marks

Barium chloride ($\mathrm{BaCl_2}$) is a water-soluble salt that is highly toxic to humans, with a lethal oral dose of approximately $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ ions. Barium sulfate ($\mathrm{BaSO_4}$), however, is swallowed in large amounts ($100\text{ g}$) as an X-ray radiocontrast agent without toxic effects.

(a) Calculate the concentration of $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ ions in a saturated solution of $\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$ given $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{BaSO_4}) = 1.08 \times 10^{-10}$. (2 Marks)
(b) Calculate the volume of water in litres a patient would need to ingest to absorb a lethal $1.0\text{ g}$ dose of $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ from a saturated $\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ solution. ($M(\mathrm{Ba}) = 137.33\text{ g mol}^{-1}$) (3 Marks)

Hint: Find $s = \sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}$. $[\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}] = s$. Find moles in $1.0\text{ g}$ $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$, then $V = n / s$.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
(a) Equilibrium Concentration of $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$: $$\mathrm{BaSO_{4(s)} \rightleftharpoons Ba^{2+}_{(aq)} + SO_{4(aq)}^{2-}}$$ $$K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}][\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}] = s^2$$ $$[\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}] = s = \sqrt{1.08 \times 10^{-10}} = \mathbf{1.039 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}}$$
(b) Required Water Volume for Lethal Dose:
1. Moles of $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ in $1.0\text{ g}$: $$n(\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}) = \frac{1.0\text{ g}}{137.33\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 7.2817 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$$
2. Calculate required volume $V$: $$V = \frac{n}{[\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}]} = \frac{7.2817 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}}{1.039 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol L}^{-1}} = \mathbf{701\text{ Litres}}$$
3. Clinical Conclusion: A patient would need to consume over $700\text{ L}$ of saturated solution to receive a toxic dose, proving why $\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ is clinically completely non-toxic and safe!

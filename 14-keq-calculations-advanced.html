1. The Reaction Quotient ($Q$): Definition and Fundamental Distinction

Quantitative Foundation

In Lessons 12 and 13, we evaluated the equilibrium constant expression $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ strictly using species concentrations measured at dynamic equilibrium. However, in laboratory synthesis and industrial chemical reactors, reaction mixtures are frequently prepared with arbitrary initial amounts of reactants and products that have not yet reached equilibrium.

To determine whether a chemical system is at equilibrium, or to predict which direction it must shift to reach equilibrium, we evaluate the Reaction Quotient ($Q$ or $Q_c$).

Formal Definition of the Reaction Quotient ($Q$)

The Reaction Quotient ($Q$) is a mathematical expression with the exact same algebraic form as the equilibrium constant expression ($K_{\mathrm{eq}}$), but evaluated using species concentrations measured at ANY given moment in time (initial, arbitrary, or non-equilibrium conditions):

$$Q = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]_{\text{current}}^c [\mathrm{D}]_{\text{current}}^d}{[\mathrm{A}]_{\text{current}}^a [\mathrm{B}]_{\text{current}}^b}$$

Where $[\text{X}]_{\text{current}}$ represents the instantaneous molar concentration ($\text{mol L}^{-1}$) of species $\text{X}$ in the reaction vessel at time $t$.

Comparing $Q$ and $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$: Key Conceptual Differences

Property Reaction Quotient ($Q$) Equilibrium Constant ($K_{\mathrm{eq}}$)
Time Domain Evaluated at any arbitrary instant ($t \ge 0$), including initial mixing ($t_0$). Evaluated strictly at dynamic equilibrium ($t \ge t_{\text{eq}}$).
Numerical Value Status Variable — Continually changes as reactants are consumed and products are formed. Constant — Fixed numerical value for a given reaction at a specific temperature.
Physical Meaning Measures the instantaneous state of the mixture relative to equilibrium. Measures the ultimate thermodynamic balance of products to reactants at equilibrium.
Role in Analysis Compared against $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ to determine the direction of spontaneous shift. Serves as the target thermodynamic benchmark that $Q$ approaches over time.

2. Comparing $Q$ against $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$: Predicting Directional Shifts

Core Decision Framework

By comparing the numerical value of $Q$ calculated from instantaneous species concentrations with the fixed value of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ at that temperature, we can unequivocally predict the direction in which the net reaction will proceed.

Case 1: $Q < K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ (Net Forward Shift $\to$)

• Physical Condition: The current ratio of products to reactants is too small relative to the equilibrium requirement.
• Thermodynamic Drive: The system must increase product concentrations and decrease reactant concentrations.
• Directional Shift: Net reaction shifts to the RIGHT ($\to$) (Forward reaction dominates: $r_{\mathrm{f}} > r_{\mathrm{r}}$).

Case 2: $Q > K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ (Net Reverse Shift $\leftarrow$)

• Physical Condition: The current ratio of products to reactants is too large relative to the equilibrium requirement.
• Thermodynamic Drive: The system must consume excess products and regenerate reactants.
• Directional Shift: Net reaction shifts to the LEFT ($\leftarrow$) (Reverse reaction dominates: $r_{\mathrm{r}} > r_{\mathrm{f}}$).

Case 3: $Q = K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ (System at Dynamic Equilibrium)

• Physical Condition: The current species concentration ratio perfectly matches the thermodynamic equilibrium constant.
• Thermodynamic Drive: No net change in species concentrations occurs over time ($\Delta c = 0$).
• Kinetic Status: The forward reaction rate equals the reverse reaction rate ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$). The system is in dynamic equilibrium.

3. Collision Theory Rationale for Directional Shifts

Kinetic Explanation

While $Q$ vs $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ comparisons provide a rigorous mathematical prediction, NESA extended-response questions require students to explain WHY the system shifts direction using Collision Theory.

Collision Mechanics when $Q < K_{\mathrm{eq}}$

When $Q < K_{\mathrm{eq}}$, the concentrations of reactant species are higher than their equilibrium values, while product concentrations are lower.

1. Higher reactant concentrations increase the frequency of successful collisions between reactant molecules per unit time.
2. Consequently, the rate of the forward reaction exceeds the rate of the reverse reaction ($r_{\mathrm{f}} > r_{\mathrm{r}}$).
3. As reactants are converted into products, reactant collision frequency decreases while product collision frequency increases until $r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$ and $Q = K_{\mathrm{eq}}$.

Collision Mechanics when $Q > K_{\mathrm{eq}}$

When $Q > K_{\mathrm{eq}}$, the concentrations of product species are higher than their equilibrium values, while reactant concentrations are lower.

1. Higher product concentrations increase the frequency of successful collisions between product molecules per unit time.
2. Consequently, the rate of the reverse reaction exceeds the rate of the forward reaction ($r_{\mathrm{r}} > r_{\mathrm{f}}$).
3. As products decompose back into reactants, product collision frequency decreases while reactant collision frequency increases until $r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$ and $Q = K_{\mathrm{eq}}$.

4. NESA Algebraic Techniques for Solving ICE Systems

HSC Exam Simplifications

In basic ICE calculations (Lesson 13), species concentrations were evaluated directly from initial values. In NESA HSC examination questions, you are expected to solve equilibrium problems efficiently using specific algebraic techniques.

Important Clarification: The Quadratic Formula in NESA HSC Chemistry

The quadratic formula ($x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$) is a general mathematical tool for solving second-order equations. However, the quadratic formula is NOT an explicit requirement of the NESA Stage 6 Chemistry syllabus.

NESA HSC Chemistry examination questions are intentionally constructed so that you do NOT need to solve complex quadratic polynomials. Instead, NESA expects you to apply one of two standard algebraic shortcuts:
1. The Perfect Square Shortcut (Square Root Method)
2. The Small-$x$ Concentration Approximation ($x \ll C$)

Shortcut Method 1: The Perfect Square Shortcut (Square Root Method)

When an equilibrium system has identical stoichiometric ratios on reactant and product sides (e.g. $\mathrm{H_{2(g)} + I_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2HI_{(g)}}$), substituting ICE terms into $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ produces a perfect square in both the numerator and denominator: $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{(C_1 + 2x)^2}{(C_2 - x)^2}$$

Instead of expanding this into a quadratic polynomial, simply take the square root of both sides: $$\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{eq}}} = \frac{C_1 + 2x}{C_2 - x}$$

This immediately reduces the equation to a simple linear equation that can be solved directly for $x$ in a few quick steps!

Shortcut Method 2: The Small-$x$ Concentration Approximation ($x \ll C$)

In many equilibrium systems (such as weak acid/base ionisation or sparingly soluble salt dissolution), the magnitude of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is very small ($K_{\mathrm{eq}} \ll 1$, typically $K_{\mathrm{eq}} < 10^{-4}$).

Because $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is so small, the extent of reaction $x$ is negligible compared to the initial concentration $C_0$ ($x \ll C_0$).

Applying and Justifying the Small-$x$ Approximation

When $x \ll C_0$, we can make the standard assumption: $$C_0 - x \approx C_0 \quad \text{and} \quad C_0 + x \approx C_0$$

Example: If $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{x^2}{0.10 - x} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, since $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is very small, we assume $0.10 - x \approx 0.10$: $$\frac{x^2}{0.10} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5} \implies x^2 = 1.8 \times 10^{-6} \implies x = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-6}} = 1.34 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$

The 5% Rule Validation: To ensure the assumption is valid, check that the calculated change $x$ is less than 5% of the initial concentration: $$\text{Percentage Change} = \frac{x}{C_0} \times 100\% = \frac{1.34 \times 10^{-3}}{0.10} \times 100\% = 1.34\% < 5\%$$ Since $1.34\% < 5\%$, the small-$x$ approximation is completely valid and earns full marks in HSC marking guidelines!

Extension Method: The Quadratic Formula (Optional Method)

Solving an ICE problem via the quadratic formula ($x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$) remains a mathematically valid technique. You may encounter it in extension questions or university-level chemistry.

If you choose to use the quadratic formula:
1. Rearrange $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ into $a x^2 + b x + c = 0$.
2. Solve for roots $x_1$ and $x_2$.
3. Reject the non-physical root with an explicit written statement (e.g. "Reject $x = -1.25\text{ M}$ as it produces a negative concentration for species X").

5. Step-by-Step Protocol for HSC $Q$ & $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Problems

Standardised HSC Method
  1. Step 1: Convert All Species to Molar Concentrations ($c = \frac{n}{V}$):
    Ensure all initial mole values are divided by the vessel volume in litres ($V$) before evaluating $Q$ or constructing the ICE table.
  2. Step 2: Calculate the Initial Reaction Quotient ($Q_{\text{init}}$):
    Substitute initial concentrations $[\text{Species}]_0$ into the mass action expression to find $Q_{\text{init}}$.
  3. Step 3: Compare $Q_{\text{init}}$ against $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ to Assign Algebraic Signs ($+$ and $-$):
    • If $Q_{\text{init}} < K_{\mathrm{eq}}$: Shift is RIGHT ($\to$). Reactants change by $-ax$, products change by $+cx$.
    • If $Q_{\text{init}} > K_{\mathrm{eq}}$: Shift is LEFT ($\leftarrow$). Reactants change by $+ax$, products change by $-cx$.
  4. Step 4: Construct the ICE Table in terms of $x$:
    Write expressions for $[\text{Species}]_{\text{eq}} = [\text{Species}]_0 \pm \text{coeff} \cdot x$.
  5. Step 5: Apply NESA Algebraic Shortcuts (Perfect Square or Small-$x$ Approximation):
    • If numerator and denominator are squared, take $\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{eq}}}$ of both sides.
    • If $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is very small ($K_{\mathrm{eq}} \ll 1$), assume $C_0 \pm x \approx C_0$ and verify the 5% rule.
  6. Step 6: Calculate Equilibrium Concentrations and State Final Answer:
    Substitute $x$ back into the equilibrium expressions and state final concentrations to correct significant figures.

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Evaluating $Q$ to Predict Directional Shift

2 Marks

At $448^\circ\text{C}$, $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = 50.0$ for the reaction $\mathrm{H_{2(g)} + I_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2HI_{(g)}}$.
A $1.00\text{ L}$ rigid vessel contains $0.200\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{H_{2(g)}}$, $0.200\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{I_{2(g)}}$, and $2.00\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{HI_{(g)}}$.
Calculate $Q$ and predict whether the system will shift forward, reverse, or remain unchanged.

Model Solution:

1. Calculate initial molar concentrations ($V = 1.00\text{ L}$):
• $[\mathrm{H_2}]_0 = 0.200\text{ M}$, $[\mathrm{I_2}]_0 = 0.200\text{ M}$, $[\mathrm{HI}]_0 = 2.00\text{ M}$.

2. Substitute initial values into the $Q$ expression: $$Q = \frac{[\mathrm{HI}]_0^2}{[\mathrm{H_2}]_0 [\mathrm{I_2}]_0} = \frac{(2.00)^2}{(0.200)(0.200)} = \frac{4.00}{0.0400} = 100$$

3. Compare $Q$ against $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ and state prediction:
Since $Q (100) > K_{\mathrm{eq}} (50.0)$, the current ratio of products to reactants is too large.
The system will shift in the REVERSE direction (to the left $\leftarrow$) to consume excess $\mathrm{HI}$ and form more $\mathrm{H_2}$ and $\mathrm{I_2}$ until equilibrium is restored.

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Directional Shift with Volume Scaling

3 Marks

For the reaction $\mathrm{2NO_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons N_2O_{4(g)}}$, $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = 8.00$ at $298\text{ K}$.
A $2.00\text{ L}$ container is charged with $3.00\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{NO_{2(g)}}$ and $2.00\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{N_2O_{4(g)}}$.
Determine $Q$ and state the direction of net reaction required to establish dynamic equilibrium.

Model Solution:

1. Convert initial moles to molar concentrations ($c = \frac{n}{V}$):
• $[\mathrm{NO_2}]_0 = \frac{3.00\text{ mol}}{2.00\text{ L}} = 1.50\text{ mol L}^{-1}$
• $[\mathrm{N_2O_4}]_0 = \frac{2.00\text{ mol}}{2.00\text{ L}} = 1.00\text{ mol L}^{-1}$

2. Calculate $Q$: $$Q = \frac{[\mathrm{N_2O_4}]_0}{[\mathrm{NO_2}]_0^2} = \frac{1.00}{(1.50)^2} = \frac{1.00}{2.25} = 0.444$$

3. Compare $Q$ and $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$:
Since $Q (0.444) < K_{\mathrm{eq}} (8.00)$, the concentration of product $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$ is insufficient relative to reactant $\mathrm{NO_2}$.
The system will shift in the FORWARD direction (to the right $\to$) to produce more $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$.

Worked Example 3 (Hard): ICE Solution Using the Small-$x$ Approximation ($x \ll C$)

4 Marks

At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = 1.80 \times 10^{-5}$ for the weak dissociation system: $\mathrm{HA_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons H^+_{(aq)} + A^-_{(aq)}}$.
A solution is prepared with an initial concentration of $[\mathrm{HA}]_0 = 0.200\text{ mol L}^{-1}$, with zero initial product ions.
Calculate the equilibrium concentration of $\mathrm{H^+_{(aq)}}$ using the small-$x$ approximation, and verify the validity of the assumption.

Model Solution:

1. Construct the ICE Table:

ICE Row $\mathrm{HA_{(aq)}}$ $\mathrm{H^+_{(aq)}}$ $\mathrm{A^-_{(aq)}}$
Initial ($\text{M}$) $0.200$ $0.00$ $0.00$
Change ($\text{M}$) $-x$ $+x$ $+x$
Equilibrium ($\text{M}$) $0.200 - x$ $x$ $x$

2. Substitute into $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ expression: $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} = \frac{x^2}{0.200 - x} = 1.80 \times 10^{-5}$$

3. Apply the Small-$x$ Approximation ($x \ll 0.200\text{ M}$):
Since $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is very small ($10^{-5} \ll 1$), assume $0.200 - x \approx 0.200$: $$\frac{x^2}{0.200} = 1.80 \times 10^{-5} \implies x^2 = 3.60 \times 10^{-6}$$ $$x = \sqrt{3.60 \times 10^{-6}} = \mathbf{1.90 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}}$$

4. Verify the 5% Rule Assumption: $$\text{Percentage Change} = \frac{1.90 \times 10^{-3}}{0.200} \times 100\% = 0.95\% < 5\%$$
Since $0.95\% < 5\%$, the small-$x$ approximation is completely valid!
Therefore, $[\mathrm{H^+}]_{\text{eq}} = \mathbf{1.90 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol L}^{-1}}$.

Worked Example 4 (Extension / HSC Challenge): Perfect Square Shortcut for System with Simultaneous Non-Zero Starting Concentrations

5 Marks

Consider the synthesis reaction: $\mathrm{CO_{(g)} + H_2O_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons CO_{2(g)} + H_{2(g)}}$, with $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = 9.00$ at $700\text{ K}$.
A $2.00\text{ L}$ rigid vessel is initially charged with $2.00\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{CO}$, $2.00\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $4.00\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and $4.00\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{H_2}$.
(a) Calculate $Q$ and state the direction of net reaction required to reach dynamic equilibrium. (2 Marks)
(b) Calculate the equilibrium concentration of $\mathrm{CO_{2(g)}}$ using the perfect square shortcut. (3 Marks)

Model Solution:

(a) Calculate $Q$ and Predict Direction:
1. Initial molar concentrations ($V = 2.00\text{ L}$):
• $[\mathrm{CO}]_0 = [\mathrm{H_2O}]_0 = \frac{2.00\text{ mol}}{2.00\text{ L}} = 1.00\text{ M}$
• $[\mathrm{CO_2}]_0 = [\mathrm{H_2}]_0 = \frac{4.00\text{ mol}}{2.00\text{ L}} = 2.00\text{ M}$
2. Substitute into $Q$ expression: $$Q = \frac{[\mathrm{CO_2}]_0 [\mathrm{H_2}]_0}{[\mathrm{CO}]_0 [\mathrm{H_2O}]_0} = \frac{(2.00)(2.00)}{(1.00)(1.00)} = 4.00$$
3. Compare $Q$ with $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$:
Since $Q (4.00) < K_{\mathrm{eq}} (9.00)$, product concentration is insufficient. The reaction shifts in the FORWARD direction ($\to$) to form more $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and $\mathrm{H_2}$.

(b) Calculate Equilibrium Concentration of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ using Perfect Square Shortcut:
1. Construct ICE Table ($+x$ for products, $-x$ for reactants):

ICE Row $\mathrm{CO_{(g)}}$ $\mathrm{H_2O_{(g)}}$ $\mathrm{CO_{2(g)}}$ $\mathrm{H_{2(g)}}$
Initial ($\text{M}$) $1.00$ $1.00$ $2.00$ $2.00$
Change ($\text{M}$) $-x$ $-x$ $+x$ $+x$
Equilibrium ($\text{M}$) $1.00 - x$ $1.00 - x$ $2.00 + x$ $2.00 + x$

2. Substitute into $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ and apply Square Root Shortcut: $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{(2.00 + x)^2}{(1.00 - x)^2} = 9.00$$ Take square root of both sides: $$\frac{2.00 + x}{1.00 - x} = \sqrt{9.00} = 3.00$$ $$2.00 + x = 3.00(1.00 - x) \implies 2.00 + x = 3.00 - 3.00x$$ $$4.00x = 1.00 \implies x = 0.250\text{ mol L}^{-1}$$
3. Final Equilibrium Concentration of $\mathrm{CO_2}$: $$[\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{eq}} = 2.00 + 0.250 = \mathbf{2.25\text{ mol L}^{-1}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

Question 4 (HSC Challenge / Extension): Multistep Reaction Quotient, Perfect Square ICE, & Mass Calculation

5 Marks

A $5.00\text{ L}$ industrial vessel contains a mixture of $\mathrm{CO_{(g)}}$, $\mathrm{H_2O_{(g)}}$, $\mathrm{CO_{2(g)}}$, and $\mathrm{H_{2(g)}}$ at $700\text{ K}$, where $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = 9.00$ for: $$\mathrm{CO_{(g)} + H_2O_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons CO_{2(g)} + H_{2(g)}}$$ The vessel initially contains $10.0\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{CO}$, $10.0\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $20.0\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and $20.0\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{H_2}$.
(a) Determine $Q$ and prove that the system is not at equilibrium. (2 Marks)
(b) Calculate the final equilibrium mass of $\mathrm{CO_{2(g)}}$ present in the vessel ($M(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 44.01\text{ g mol}^{-1}$). (3 Marks)

Hint: $[\mathrm{CO}]_0 = [\mathrm{H_2O}]_0 = 2.00\text{ M}$, $[\mathrm{CO_2}]_0 = [\mathrm{H_2}]_0 = 4.00\text{ M}$. $Q = \frac{(4.00)^2}{(2.00)^2} = 4.00 < 9.00$. Use square root shortcut on ICE expression.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
(a) Calculate $Q$:
• $[\mathrm{CO}]_0 = [\mathrm{H_2O}]_0 = \frac{10.0}{5.00} = 2.00\text{ M}$
• $[\mathrm{CO_2}]_0 = [\mathrm{H_2}]_0 = \frac{20.0}{5.00} = 4.00\text{ M}$ $$Q = \frac{(4.00)(4.00)}{(2.00)(2.00)} = \frac{16.0}{4.00} = 4.00$$ Since $Q (4.00) \neq K_{\mathrm{eq}} (9.00)$, the system is not at equilibrium. Since $Q < K_{\mathrm{eq}}$, it will shift FORWARD ($\to$).

(b) Calculate Equilibrium Mass of $\mathrm{CO_2}$:
1. ICE Expressions: $[\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{eq}} = 4.00 + x$, $[\mathrm{CO}]_{\text{eq}} = 2.00 - x$.
2. Substitute into $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$: $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{(4.00 + x)^2}{(2.00 - x)^2} = 9.00$$
3. Take Square Root: $$\frac{4.00 + x}{2.00 - x} = \sqrt{9.00} = 3.00$$ $$4.00 + x = 3.00(2.00 - x) \implies 4.00 + x = 6.00 - 3.00x \implies 4.00x = 2.00 \implies x = 0.500\text{ M}$$
4. Calculate $[\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{eq}}$ and Total Moles:
• $[\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{eq}} = 4.00 + 0.500 = 4.50\text{ mol L}^{-1}$
• $n(\mathrm{CO_2})_{\text{eq}} = c \times V = 4.50\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 5.00\text{ L} = 22.5\text{ mol}$
5. Calculate Mass: $$m(\mathrm{CO_2}) = n \times M = 22.5\text{ mol} \times 44.01\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{990\text{ g}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

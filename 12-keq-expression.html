1. The Law of Mass Action and Kinetic Derivation

Foundational Theory

In Phase B, we used Le Chatelier's Principle to qualitatively predict whether a disturbed equilibrium system shifts left ($\leftarrow$) or right ($\to$). However, qualitative rules cannot quantify the exact balance of reactants and products remaining when dynamic equilibrium is established.

In 1864, Norwegian chemists Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage formulated the Law of Mass Action. They discovered empirically that for any reversible chemical reaction at a constant temperature, a specific mathematical ratio of reactant and product concentrations remains perfectly constant once dynamic equilibrium is attained, regardless of the initial starting concentrations!

Kinetic Derivation for an Elementary Homogeneous System

Consider a simple reversible gas-phase reaction occurring in a single step (elementary reaction):

$$\mathrm{a A_{(g)} + b B_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons c C_{(g)} + d D_{(g)}}$$

According to kinetic rate theory, the rate of the forward reaction ($r_{\mathrm{f}}$) and reverse reaction ($r_{\mathrm{r}}$) can be written as:

$$r_{\mathrm{f}} = k_{\mathrm{f}} [\mathrm{A}]^a [\mathrm{B}]^b \quad \text{and} \quad r_{\mathrm{r}} = k_{\mathrm{r}} [\mathrm{C}]^c [\mathrm{D}]^d$$

Where $k_{\mathrm{f}}$ and $k_{\mathrm{r}}$ are the forward and reverse rate constants at a specific temperature.

By definition, dynamic equilibrium is established when the forward rate equals the reverse rate ($r_{\mathrm{f}} = r_{\mathrm{r}}$):

$$k_{\mathrm{f}} [\mathrm{A}]_{\text{eq}}^a [\mathrm{B}]_{\text{eq}}^b = k_{\mathrm{r}} [\mathrm{C}]_{\text{eq}}^c [\mathrm{D}]_{\text{eq}}^d$$

Rearranging this equality to isolate the ratio of rate constants ($\frac{k_{\mathrm{f}}}{k_{\mathrm{r}}}$) on one side gives:

$$\frac{k_{\mathrm{f}}}{k_{\mathrm{r}}} = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]_{\text{eq}}^c [\mathrm{D}]_{\text{eq}}^d}{[\mathrm{A}]_{\text{eq}}^a [\mathrm{B}]_{\text{eq}}^b}$$

Definition of the Equilibrium Constant ($K_{\mathrm{eq}}$)

Since $k_{\mathrm{f}}$ and $k_{\mathrm{r}}$ are constants at a given temperature, their ratio $\frac{k_{\mathrm{f}}}{k_{\mathrm{r}}}$ is also a constant, defined as the Equilibrium Constant ($K_{\mathrm{eq}}$): $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c [\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a [\mathrm{B}]^b}$$
• Square Brackets $[\mathrm{X}]$: Represent molar concentration in $\text{mol L}^{-1}$ at equilibrium.
• Products in Numerator: Molar concentrations of all gaseous/aqueous products raised to their stoichiometric powers.
• Reactants in Denominator: Molar concentrations of all gaseous/aqueous reactants raised to their stoichiometric powers.

2. Deducing $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Expressions for Homogeneous Systems

System Classification

A homogeneous system is defined as a chemical equilibrium system in which all reacting species (reactants and products) exist within a single physical state of matter (a single phase).

1. Homogeneous Gas-Phase Systems

All reactants and products are gases ($\text{g}$). Because gases expand uniformly throughout the vessel, every gaseous species possesses a measurable molar concentration ($[\text{X}] = \frac{n}{V}$ in $\text{mol L}^{-1}$) that varies with volume and mol count.

Example (Haber Process): $$\mathrm{N_{2(g)} + 3H_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NH_{3(g)}}$$ $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{[\mathrm{NH_3}]^2}{[\mathrm{N_2}][\mathrm{H_2}]^3}$$

2. Homogeneous Aqueous Systems

All reactants and products are dissolved solutes in water ($\text{aq}$). Every solute species possesses a variable molar concentration ($[\text{X}]$ in $\text{mol L}^{-1}$) participating in the aqueous phase equilibrium.

Example (Iron(III) Thiocyanate Complex): $$\mathrm{Fe^{3+}_{(aq)} + SCN^-_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons FeSCN^{2+}_{(aq)}}$$ $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{[\mathrm{FeSCN^{2+}}]}{[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}][\mathrm{SCN^-}]}$$

Figure 12.1: Microscopic Uniformity in Homogeneous Gas and Aqueous Systems

Golden Rules for Writing $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Expressions

1. Products Over Reactants: Always place product terms in the numerator and reactant terms in the denominator.
2. Multiplication, NOT Addition: Multiply individual species terms together (e.g., $[\mathrm{A}]^a \times [\mathrm{B}]^b$), never add them!
3. Exponents Equal Coefficients: Raising each species concentration to the power of its balanced stoichiometric coefficient in the chemical equation.
4. Equilibrium Concentrations Only: Brackets $[\text{X}]$ denote concentrations at dynamic equilibrium, NOT initial concentrations before reaction!

3. Physical Significance of the Magnitude of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$

Extent vs Rate

The numerical value of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ provides a direct quantitative measure of the extent of reaction—how far the reaction proceeds towards completion before dynamic equilibrium is established.

Numerical Value of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Dominant Species at Equilibrium Equilibrium Position Status Chemical Significance & Examples
$K_{\mathrm{eq}} \gg 10^3$
(Very Large)		 $[\text{Products}] \gg [\text{Reactants}]$ Lies FAR TO THE RIGHT ($\to$) Reaction proceeds essentially to completion. At equilibrium, reactants are almost entirely converted into products.
e.g., $\mathrm{2H_{2(g)} + O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2H_2O_{(g)}} \quad (K \approx 10^{80})$
$10^{-3} \le K_{\mathrm{eq}} \le 10^3$
(Moderate)		 $[\text{Products}] \approx [\text{Reactants}]$ APPRECIABLE AMOUNTS of both Significant concentrations of both reactants and products co-exist at dynamic equilibrium.
e.g., $\mathrm{H_{2(g)} + I_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2HI_{(g)}} \quad (K \approx 54 \text{ at } 425^\circ\text{C})$
$K_{\mathrm{eq}} \ll 10^{-3}$
(Very Small)		 $[\text{Reactants}] \gg [\text{Products}]$ Lies FAR TO THE LEFT ($\leftarrow$) Reaction proceeds to a negligible extent. Reactants remain almost completely unreacted.
e.g., $\mathrm{N_{2(g)} + O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NO_{(g)}} \quad (K \approx 10^{-30} \text{ at } 25^\circ\text{C})$

Band 6 Exam Trap: Extent vs Rate Distinction!

A common error in HSC examinations is confusing the magnitude of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ (extent) with the rate of reaction (speed).

• $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ communicates HOW FAR a reaction goes (Thermodynamics). A reaction can have an enormous $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = 10^{40}$ (extensively favoured) yet proceed at an imperceptibly slow rate due to a massive activation energy barrier ($E_a$).

• Reaction rate communicates HOW FAST equilibrium is reached (Kinetics). Rate depends on collision frequency, activation energy, and catalysts—none of which alter the final value of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$!

4. Algebraic Transformations of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Expressions

Mathematical Rules

The written form of a $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ expression is strictly tied to the specific stoichiometry and direction of the balanced chemical equation. If a chemical equation is reversed, multiplied by a coefficient factor, or summed sequentially, the corresponding $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ expression transforms according to exact mathematical operations.

Rule 1: Reversing the Reaction Equation ($K' = \frac{1}{K}$)

When a chemical equation is written in the reverse direction, products become reactants and reactants become products. The new equilibrium constant $K_{\text{rev}}$ is the reciprocal (inverse) of the forward equilibrium constant $K_{\text{fwd}}$:

$$K_{\text{rev}} = \frac{1}{K_{\text{fwd}}} = (K_{\text{fwd}})^{-1}$$

Rule 2: Multiplying Stoichiometric Coefficients by Factor $n$ ($K'' = K^n$)

If all stoichiometric coefficients in a balanced chemical equation are multiplied by a constant factor $n$ (where $n$ can be an integer or a fraction such as $\frac{1}{2}$), the new equilibrium constant $K'$ equals the original equilibrium constant raised to the power of $n$:

$$K' = (K_{\text{orig}})^n$$

Example 2a: Doubling Coefficients ($n = 2$)

Original: $\mathrm{A + B \rightleftharpoons C} \quad (K_1 = 5)$
Doubled: $\mathrm{2A + 2B \rightleftharpoons 2C}$
$$K_2 = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^2}{[\mathrm{A}]^2[\mathrm{B}]^2} = (K_1)^2 = 5^2 = 25$$

Example 2b: Halving Coefficients ($n = \frac{1}{2}$)

Original: $\mathrm{2NO_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons N_2O_{4(g)}} \quad (K_1 = 16)$
Halved: $\mathrm{NO_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \frac{1}{2}N_2O_{4(g)}}$
$$K_2 = \frac{[\mathrm{N_2O_4}]^{1/2}}{[\mathrm{NO_2}]} = (K_1)^{1/2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$

Rule 3: Combining Sequential Reaction Steps ($K_{\text{overall}} = K_1 \times K_2$)

When two or more individual equilibrium equations are added together to yield an overall net chemical equation, the overall equilibrium constant $K_{\text{overall}}$ is the PRODUCT (multiplication) of the individual equilibrium constants:

$$K_{\text{overall}} = K_1 \times K_2 \times K_3 \times \dots$$

Proof for Summing Sequential Reactions

Step 1: $\mathrm{A + B \rightleftharpoons C} \quad \implies K_1 = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]}$
Step 2: $\mathrm{C + D \rightleftharpoons E} \quad \implies K_2 = \frac{[\mathrm{E}]}{[\mathrm{C}][\mathrm{D}]}$

Net Reaction (Step 1 + Step 2): $\mathrm{A + B + D \rightleftharpoons E}$
$$K_{\text{net}} = \frac{[\mathrm{E}]}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}][\mathrm{D}]}$$
Multiplying $K_1 \times K_2$: $$K_1 \times K_2 = \left(\frac{[\mathrm{C}]}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]}\right) \times \left(\frac{[\mathrm{E}]}{[\mathrm{C}][\mathrm{D}]}\right) = \frac{[\mathrm{E}]}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}][\mathrm{D}]} = K_{\text{net}}$$

5. NESA Convention Regarding $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Units

HSC Exam Standard

In university-level thermodynamics, the equilibrium constant is formally defined in terms of thermodynamic activities ($\mathbf{a}_i = \frac{[\mathrm{X}]}{C^\circ}$, relative to standard state $C^\circ = 1\text{ mol L}^{-1}$), rendering $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ strictly dimensionless (unitless).

NESA Stage 6 Chemistry Policy: In HSC examinations, students are expected to treat $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ values as dimensionless numbers without units. You do NOT need to derive or attach units such as $\text{mol}^{-2} \text{L}^2$ to $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ values in NESA assessments. State the numerical magnitude clearly without units!

7. Scaffolded Worked Examples

HSC Step-by-Step Solutions

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Deduce Homogeneous Gas Expression

2 Marks

Write the equilibrium constant ($K_{\mathrm{eq}}$) expression for the oxidation of sulfur dioxide gas to sulfur trioxide gas: $$\mathrm{2SO_{2(g)} + O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2SO_{3(g)}}$$

Model Solution:

1. Identify products and reactants with state symbols:
• Product: $\mathrm{SO_{3(g)}}$ with coefficient 2.
• Reactants: $\mathrm{SO_{2(g)}}$ with coefficient 2, $\mathrm{O_{2(g)}}$ with coefficient 1.

2. Construct the expression (Products over Reactants with powers): $$K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{[\mathrm{SO_3}]^2}{[\mathrm{SO_2}]^2[\mathrm{O_2}]}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Reversing and Scaling Coefficients

3 Marks

At $500\text{ K}$, the equilibrium constant for $\mathrm{N_{2(g)} + 3H_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NH_{3(g)}}$ is $K_1 = 6.0 \times 10^{-2}$.
Deduce the expression and calculate the numerical value of $K_2$ at the same temperature for the reaction: $$\mathrm{NH_{3(g)} \rightleftharpoons \frac{1}{2}N_{2(g)} + \frac{3}{2}H_{2(g)}}$$

Model Solution:

1. Deduce the new $K_2$ expression: $$K_2 = \frac{[\mathrm{N_2}]^{1/2}[\mathrm{H_2}]^{3/2}}{[\mathrm{NH_3}]}$$

2. Identify transformations relative to $K_1$:
• The equation is reversed (take reciprocal $\frac{1}{K_1}$).
• The coefficients are halved ($n = \frac{1}{2}$, take square root $\sqrt{\phantom{X}}$).

3. Calculate numerical value: $$K_2 = \left(\frac{1}{K_1}\right)^{1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{K_1}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6.0 \times 10^{-2}}} = \frac{1}{0.24495} = 4.08 = 4.1 \quad (2\text{ sig. figs.})$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Two-Step Sequential Reaction Derivation

4 Marks

The atmospheric formation of nitrogen dioxide proceeds via two sequential equilibrium steps:
Step 1: $\mathrm{N_{2(g)} + O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NO_{(g)}} \quad (K_1 = 4.0 \times 10^{-31})$
Step 2: $\mathrm{2NO_{(g)} + O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NO_{2(g)}} \quad (K_2 = 1.6 \times 10^{12})$
Deduce the overall chemical equation, express its $K_{\text{overall}}$ expression, and calculate its numerical value at $25^\circ\text{C}$.

Model Solution:

1. Add individual equations to obtain net overall equation: $$\text{Step 1: } \mathrm{N_{2(g)} + O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \cancel{2NO_{(g)}}}$$ $$\text{Step 2: } \mathrm{\cancel{2NO_{(g)}} + O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NO_{2(g)}}$$ $$\text{Net Overall: } \mathrm{N_{2(g)} + 2O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NO_{2(g)}}$$

2. Deduce overall $K_{\text{overall}}$ expression: $$K_{\text{overall}} = \frac{[\mathrm{NO_2}]^2}{[\mathrm{N_2}][\mathrm{O_2}]^2}$$

3. Calculate numerical value using $K_{\text{overall}} = K_1 \times K_2$: $$K_{\text{overall}} = (4.0 \times 10^{-31}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{12}) = 6.4 \times 10^{-19}$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Non-Unity Kinetic Mechanisms & $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Bridge

5 Marks

A non-elementary gas reaction $\mathrm{2A_{(g)} + B_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2C_{(g)}}$ occurs via a two-step mechanism:
Step 1 (Fast equilibrium): $\mathrm{A_{(g)} + B_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons I_{(g)}} \quad (\text{forward rate constant } k_1, \text{ reverse } k_{-1})$
Step 2 (Slow elementary): $\mathrm{I_{(g)} + A_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2C_{(g)}} \quad (\text{forward rate constant } k_2, \text{ reverse } k_{-2})$
Prove mathematically that the microscopic ratio of overall forward and reverse rate constants $\frac{k_1 k_2}{k_{-1} k_{-2}}$ yields the standard macroscopic $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ expression.

Model Solution:

1. Write equilibrium constant expressions for individual steps:
• For Step 1: $K_1 = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}} = \frac{[\mathrm{I}]}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]}$
• For Step 2: $K_2 = \frac{k_2}{k_{-2}} = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^2}{[\mathrm{I}][\mathrm{A}]}$

2. Multiply rate constant ratios $K_1 \times K_2$: $$\frac{k_1 k_2}{k_{-1} k_{-2}} = K_1 \times K_2 = \left(\frac{[\mathrm{I}]}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]}\right) \times \left(\frac{[\mathrm{C}]^2}{[\mathrm{I}][\mathrm{A}]}\right)$$

3. Cancel intermediate $[\mathrm{I}]$: $$\frac{k_1 k_2}{k_{-1} k_{-2}} = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^2}{[\mathrm{A}]^2 [\mathrm{B}]} = K_{\mathrm{eq}}$$
This proves that regardless of multi-step mechanisms, the macroscopic $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ expression depends solely on overall balanced stoichiometry!

Question 2 (Medium): Physical Interpretation of $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Magnitude

3 Marks

At $25^\circ\text{C}$, reaction A has $K_A = 10^{35}$ while reaction B has $K_B = 10^{-15}$.
Distinguish between these two systems in terms of equilibrium position and relative concentrations of reactants and products at dynamic equilibrium.

Hint: $K \gg 1$ implies products dominate; $K \ll 1$ implies reactants dominate.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
• System A ($K_A = 10^{35} \gg 1$): The equilibrium position lies far to the right ($\to$). The reaction proceeds virtually to completion, meaning $[\text{Products}] \gg [\text{Reactants}]$ at equilibrium.
• System B ($K_B = 10^{-15} \ll 1$): The equilibrium position lies far to the left ($\leftarrow$). The reaction proceeds to a negligible extent, meaning $[\text{Reactants}] \gg [\text{Products}]$ at equilibrium.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly characterizes System A as far to the right with products dominating.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly characterizes System B as far to the left with reactants dominating.
  • 1 Mark: Uses clear comparative chemical language (extent of reaction / ratio of species).

Question 3 (Hard): Transforming $K_{\mathrm{eq}}$ Expressions and Values

4 Marks

Consider the gas equilibrium: $\mathrm{2NOCl_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2NO_{(g)} + Cl_{2(g)}} \quad (K_1 = 4.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ at } 240^\circ\text{C})$.
Deduce the expression and calculate the numerical value of $K_2$ for the reaction: $$\mathrm{NO_{(g)} + \frac{1}{2}Cl_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons NOCl_{(g)}}$$

Hint: The reaction is reversed and halved. Apply $K_2 = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K_1}}$.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
1. Deduce Expression: $$K_2 = \frac{[\mathrm{NOCl}]}{[\mathrm{NO}][\mathrm{Cl_2}]^{1/2}}$$
2. Mathematical Operations:
• The target equation is reversed ($\implies \frac{1}{K_1}$).
• The coefficients are multiplied by $\frac{1}{2}$ ($\implies \text{power of } \frac{1}{2}$ or square root).
3. Calculation: $$K_2 = \left(\frac{1}{K_1}\right)^{1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}} = \frac{1}{0.020} = 50$$

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Writes correct $K_2$ expression with correct fractional exponent.
  • 1 Mark: Identifies that equation is reversed and coefficients halved.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly applies $K_2 = (K_1)^{-1/2}$ transformation.
  • 1 Mark: Calculates correct final value of $50$ (or $5.0 \times 10^1$).

Question 4 (Extension): Multistep Equilibrium Synthesis

5 Marks

Given the following equilibrium constants at $298\text{ K}$:
(1) $\mathrm{2CO_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2CO_{(g)} + O_{2(g)}} \quad K_1 = 1.4 \times 10^{-12}$
(2) $\mathrm{2H_2O_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2H_{2(g)} + O_{2(g)}} \quad K_2 = 2.0 \times 10^{-10}$
Determine the equilibrium constant $K_3$ for the water-gas shift reaction: $$\mathrm{CO_{(g)} + H_2O_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons CO_{2(g)} + H_{2(g)}}$$

Hint: Reverse Equation (1) and halve it. Halve Equation (2). Add them together.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
1. Manipulate Equation (1):
Reverse and halve: $\mathrm{CO_{(g)} + \frac{1}{2}O_{2(g)} \rightleftharpoons CO_{2(g)}}$ $$K_{1'} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{K_1}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.4 \times 10^{-12}}} = 8.4515 \times 10^5$$
2. Manipulate Equation (2):
Halve: $\mathrm{H_2O_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons H_{2(g)} + \frac{1}{2}O_{2(g)}}$ $$K_{2'} = \sqrt{K_2} = \sqrt{2.0 \times 10^{-10}} = 1.4142 \times 10^{-5}$$
3. Add Manipulated Equations: $$\mathrm{CO_{(g)} + \cancel{\frac{1}{2}O_{2(g)}} + H_2O_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons CO_{2(g)} + H_{2(g)} + \cancel{\frac{1}{2}O_{2(g)}}}$$ $$\mathrm{CO_{(g)} + H_2O_{(g)} \rightleftharpoons CO_{2(g)} + H_{2(g)}}$$
4. Calculate $K_3 = K_{1'} \times K_{2'}$: $$K_3 = (8.4515 \times 10^5) \times (1.4142 \times 10^{-5}) = 11.95 = 12 \quad (2\text{ sig. figs.})$$

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly reverses and halves Equation (1) with $K_{1'} = (K_1)^{-1/2}$.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly halves Equation (2) with $K_{2'} = (K_2)^{1/2}$.
  • 1 Mark: Shows addition of manipulated equations yields target equation.
  • 1 Mark: Applies $K_3 = K_{1'} \times K_{2'}$.
  • 1 Mark: Obtains correct final value of $12$ (or $1.2 \times 10^1$).

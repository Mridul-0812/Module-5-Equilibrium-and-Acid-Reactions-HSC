1. The NESA Stage 6 Solubility Rules & Mnemonic Framework

Core Heuristics

In aqueous chemistry, ionic compounds exhibit vastly different degrees of solubility. While some salts dissolve completely to form concentrated solutions ($> 0.1\text{ mol L}^{-1}$), others are so sparingly soluble that mixing their constituent ions immediately produces an insoluble solid compound called a precipitate.

To accurately predict whether mixing two aqueous electrolyte solutions will yield a precipitate, HSC Chemistry students must memorise and apply the NESA Stage 6 Solubility Rules.

The NAGSAG Mnemonic for Soluble Compounds

The acronym NAGSAG summarizes the six main classes of ionic compounds that are ALWAYS SOLUBLE in water with very few specific exceptions:

  • N — Nitrates ($\mathrm{NO_3^-}$): All nitrate salts are soluble. Zero exceptions!
  • A — Acetates ($\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$): All acetate / ethanoate salts are soluble.
  • G — Group 1 Alkali Metals ($\mathrm{Li^+}, \mathrm{Na^+}, \mathrm{K^+}, \text{etc.}$): All alkali metal salts are soluble. Zero exceptions!
  • S — Sulfates ($\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$): Soluble EXCEPT when paired with $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}, \mathrm{Ba^{2+}}, \mathrm{Sr^{2+}}, \mathrm{Ca^{2+}}, \mathrm{Ag^+}$ (PMS / Castro Bear exceptions).
  • A — Ammonium ($\mathrm{NH_4^+}$): All ammonium salts are soluble. Zero exceptions!
  • G — Group 17 Halides ($\mathrm{Cl^-}, \mathrm{Br^-}, \mathrm{I^-}$): Soluble EXCEPT when paired with $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ or $\mathrm{Ag^+}$ (PMS exceptions).

2. Sub-Microscopic Mechanism of Precipitation

Dynamic Collision Kinetics

When two clear aqueous salt solutions are poured together, why does a cloudy solid precipitate form almost instantaneously?

Prior to mixing, each starting solution contains fully dissociated, mobile hydrated cations and anions surrounded by water hydration shells. For instance, mixing aqueous silver nitrate ($\mathrm{AgNO_{3(aq)}}$) and sodium chloride ($\mathrm{NaCl_{(aq)}}$) introduces four mobile ion species into a single vessel: $$\mathrm{Ag^+_{(aq)}}, \quad \mathrm{NO_{3(aq)}^-}, \quad \mathrm{Na^+_{(aq)}}, \quad \mathrm{Cl^-_{(aq)}}$$

1. Random Thermal Collision

Hydrated ions move randomly throughout the liquid phase driven by thermal kinetic energy. As solution mixing occurs, ion collisions occur rapidly across all combination pairs ($\mathrm{Ag^+}$ with $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, and $\mathrm{Na^+}$ with $\mathrm{NO_3^-}$).

2. Electrostatic Competition

For $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{NO_3^-}$, the ion-dipole attraction with solvent water molecules is significantly stronger than the electrostatic attraction between the ions themselves. They remain hydrated and mobile.

3. Lattice Nucleation & Precipitation

For $\mathrm{Ag^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, the mutual electrostatic attraction between opposite ionic charges is immensely powerful ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{lattice}}$ is extremely negative). Upon collision, the ion-ion attraction overcomes the hydration shells, expelling water molecules to form solid crystalline nuclei: $$\mathrm{Ag^+_{(aq)} + Cl^-_{(aq)} \to AgCl_{(s)}}$$

3. Writing Chemical Equations for Precipitation

Formal Equation Hierarchy

In HSC Chemistry examinations, you are required to write three distinct levels of chemical equations to represent precipitation reactions. Mastering the step-by-step conversion between these levels is critical for securing full marks.

The Three Equation Levels (Illustrated with $\mathrm{Pb(NO_3)_2}$ + $\mathrm{KI}$)

1. Full Formula (Molecular) Equation

Shows all reactants and products written as neutral chemical formulas with correct stoichiometric balancing and state symbols ($\text{aq}$ or $\text{s}$): $$\mathrm{Pb(NO_3)_{2(aq)} + 2KI_{(aq)} \to PbI_{2(s)} + 2KNO_{3(aq)}}$$

2. Complete Ionic Equation

Expands all strong soluble electrolytes ($\text{aq}$) into their individual dissociated aqueous ions, while keeping insoluble solid precipitates ($\text{s}$) together as intact chemical formulas: $$\mathrm{Pb^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2NO_{3(aq)}^- + 2K^+_{(aq)} + 2I^-_{(aq)} \to PbI_{2(s)} + 2K^+_{(aq)} + 2NO_{3(aq)}^-}$$

3. Net Ionic Equation

Cancels out all spectator ions (species that appear unchanged on both sides of the reaction arrow in the aqueous phase) to highlight the essential chemical change: $$\mathrm{Pb^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2I^-_{(aq)} \to PbI_{2(s)}}$$

Spectator Ions: Definition & Diagnostic Rules

Spectator ions are ionic species that remain dissolved in the aqueous solution throughout the course of a chemical reaction without undergoing any change in oxidation state, physical state, or chemical bonding.

  • In the reaction above, $\mathrm{K^+_{(aq)}}$ and $\mathrm{NO_{3(aq)}^-}$ enter as mobile aqueous ions and remain as mobile aqueous ions. They do not participate in forming the $\mathrm{PbI_{2(s)}}$ crystal lattice.
  • Exam Rule: Spectator ions MUST be completely omitted when asked to write a net ionic equation! Including spectator ions in a net ionic equation is a frequent source of mark deduction in HSC exams.

4. Qualitative Precipitation Protocols & Gravimetric Foundations

Analytical Flowcharts

A classic application of solubility rules in NESA Module 5 is designing a systematic qualitative analysis scheme to identify or separate specific metal cations or anions present in an unknown aqueous sample.

Golden Rules for Designing Sequential Separation Flowcharts

  1. Add Reagents Sequentially: Add a precipitation reagent that reacts selectively with ONLY ONE target cation in the mixture, leaving all other cations dissolved.
  2. Filtration Step: After precipitating the first ion, the mixture MUST be filtered to physically remove the solid precipitate. If unfiltered, adding subsequent testing reagents will cause confusing secondary side-reactions with the solid!
  3. Test Filtrate for Completeness: Ensure all of the target ion has been completely precipitated before adding the next analytical reagent.

Example Protocol: Separating $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$, $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$, and $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ Cations

Suppose a solution contains a mixture of $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}_{(aq)}}$, $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}_{(aq)}}$, and $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}_{(aq)}}$ cations.

  1. Step 1 (Precipitate $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$): Add dilute hydrochloric acid ($\mathrm{HCl_{(aq)}}$).
    • $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ reacts to form a white precipitate: $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2Cl^-_{(aq)} \to PbCl_{2(s)}}$.
    • $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ remain fully soluble as chlorides.
    • Action: Filter off the $\mathrm{PbCl_{2(s)}}$ solid residue. Collect the liquid filtrate.
  2. Step 2 (Precipitate $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$): To the clear filtrate, add sodium sulfate solution ($\mathrm{Na_2SO_{4(aq)}}$).
    • $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ reacts to form a dense white precipitate: $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}_{(aq)} + SO_{4(aq)}^{2-} \to BaSO_{4(s)}}$.
    • $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ remains fully soluble ($\mathrm{CuSO_4}$ is soluble).
    • Action: Filter off the $\mathrm{BaSO_{4(s)}}$ solid residue.
  3. Step 3 (Precipitate / Confirm $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$): To the remaining filtrate, add sodium hydroxide ($\mathrm{NaOH_{(aq)}}$).
    • $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ reacts to form a characteristic blue precipitate: $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2OH^-_{(aq)} \to Cu(OH)_{2(s)}}$.

6. Scaffolded Worked Examples

HSC Step-by-Step Solutions

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Predicting Solubility using NESA Rules

2 Marks

Using standard NESA solubility rules, classify each of the following four ionic compounds as either soluble or insoluble in water at $25^\circ\text{C}$:
(a) Barium nitrate ($\mathrm{Ba(NO_3)_2}$)
(b) Lead(II) chloride ($\mathrm{PbCl_2}$)
(c) Ammonium carbonate ($(\mathrm{NH_4)_2CO_3}$)
(d) Copper(II) hydroxide ($\mathrm{Cu(OH)_2}$)

Model Solution:

(a) Barium nitrate ($\mathrm{Ba(NO_3)_2}$): SOLUBLE. Nitrates ($\mathrm{NO_3^-}$) are unconditionally soluble without exception.

(b) Lead(II) chloride ($\mathrm{PbCl_2}$): INSOLUBLE. Most chlorides are soluble, but $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ chloride is a key exception and precipitates as a white solid.

(c) Ammonium carbonate ($(\mathrm{NH_4)_2CO_3}$): SOLUBLE. Although most carbonates are insoluble, all ammonium ($\mathrm{NH_4^+}$) salts are soluble without exception.

(d) Copper(II) hydroxide ($\mathrm{Cu(OH)_2}$): INSOLUBLE. Most transition metal hydroxides are insoluble in water, forming a pale blue precipitate.

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Writing Full, Ionic, and Net Ionic Equations

3 Marks

An aqueous solution of sodium hydroxide ($\mathrm{NaOH_{(aq)}}$) is added to an aqueous solution of iron(III) nitrate ($\mathrm{Fe(NO_3)_{3(aq)}}$). A dark rust-brown precipitate forms immediately.

Write balanced chemical equations for this reaction:
(a) Full formula (molecular) equation (1 Mark)
(b) Complete ionic equation (1 Mark)
(c) Net ionic equation (1 Mark)

Model Solution:

(a) Full Formula Equation: $$\mathrm{Fe(NO_3)_{3(aq)} + 3NaOH_{(aq)} \to Fe(OH)_{3(s)} + 3NaNO_{3(aq)}}$$

(b) Complete Ionic Equation: $$\mathrm{Fe^{3+}_{(aq)} + 3NO_{3(aq)}^- + 3Na^+_{(aq)} + 3OH^-_{(aq)} \to Fe(OH)_{3(s)} + 3Na^+_{(aq)} + 3NO_{3(aq)}^-}$$

(c) Net Ionic Equation: $$\mathrm{Fe^{3+}_{(aq)} + 3OH^-_{(aq)} \to Fe(OH)_{3(s)}}$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Identifying Spectator Ions and Predicting Mass Changes

4 Marks

A student mixes $100.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.200\text{ M}$ barium chloride solution ($\mathrm{BaCl_{2(aq)}}$) with $100.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.200\text{ M}$ sodium sulfate solution ($\mathrm{Na_2SO_{4(aq)}}$).

(a) Identify the spectator ions present in the final mixture. (1 Mark)
(b) Write the net ionic equation for the reaction. (1 Mark)
(c) Calculate the theoretical mass of solid precipitate formed after filtering and drying. (2 Marks)

Model Solution:

(a) Spectator Ions:
The spectator ions are $\mathrm{Na^+_{(aq)}}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-_{(aq)}}$.

(b) Net Ionic Equation: $$\mathrm{Ba^{2+}_{(aq)} + SO_{4(aq)}^{2-} \to BaSO_{4(s)}}$$

(c) Mass Calculation:
1. Calculate moles of $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$: $$n(\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}) = c \times V = 0.200\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 0.1000\text{ L} = 0.0200\text{ mol}$$ $$n(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}) = c \times V = 0.200\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 0.1000\text{ L} = 0.0200\text{ mol}$$
2. Both reactants are in exact $1:1$ stoichiometric proportion, producing $0.0200\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{BaSO_{4(s)}}$.
3. Molar mass of $\mathrm{BaSO_4} = 137.33 + 32.06 + (4 \times 16.00) = 233.39\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
4. Mass of precipitate: $$m = n \times M = 0.0200\text{ mol} \times 233.39\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{4.67\text{ g}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Designing a Cation Separation Flowchart

5 Marks

An unlabelled aqueous solution is known to contain a mixture of $\mathrm{Ag^+_{(aq)}}$, $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}_{(aq)}}$, and $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}_{(aq)}}$ cations at concentrations of $0.10\text{ M}$.

Design a step-by-step experimental procedure to sequentially separate and isolate all three cations as solid precipitates. For each step, state the reagent added, the net ionic equation, and the necessary physical separation technique.

Model Solution:

Step 1 (Isolate $\mathrm{Ag^+}$):
• Reagent: Add excess dilute sodium chloride solution ($\mathrm{NaCl_{(aq)}}$).
• Net Ionic Equation: $\mathrm{Ag^+_{(aq)} + Cl^-_{(aq)} \to AgCl_{(s)}}$ (white precipitate).
• Separation: Filter the mixture to collect solid $\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)}}$ residue. Retain filtrate containing $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$.

Step 2 (Isolate $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$):
• Reagent: To the filtrate, add excess sodium sulfate solution ($\mathrm{Na_2SO_{4(aq)}}$).
• Net Ionic Equation: $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}_{(aq)} + SO_{4(aq)}^{2-} \to BaSO_{4(s)}}$ (dense white precipitate).
• Separation: Filter the mixture to collect solid $\mathrm{BaSO_{4(s)}}$ residue. Retain filtrate containing $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$.

Step 3 (Isolate $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$):
• Reagent: To the remaining filtrate, add excess sodium hydroxide solution ($\mathrm{NaOH_{(aq)}}$).
• Net Ionic Equation: $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2OH^-_{(aq)} \to Mg(OH)_{2(s)}}$ (white gel-like precipitate).
• Separation: Filter to collect solid $\mathrm{Mg(OH)_{2(s)}}$ residue.

Question 3 (Hard): Stoichiometric Precipitation Calculation

4 Marks

A technician mixes $250.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.150\text{ M}$ silver nitrate solution ($\mathrm{AgNO_{3(aq)}}$) with $150.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.200\text{ M}$ magnesium chloride solution ($\mathrm{MgCl_{2(aq)}}$).

Calculate the mass of silver chloride precipitate formed, identifying any limiting reagent.

Hint: $n(\mathrm{Ag^+}) = c \times V$. Note that 1 mole of $\mathrm{MgCl_2}$ yields 2 moles of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$! Calculate $n(\mathrm{Cl^-}) = 2 \times c \times V$.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
1. Net ionic equation: $$\mathrm{Ag^+_{(aq)} + Cl^-_{(aq)} \to AgCl_{(s)}}$$
2. Calculate initial moles of ions: $$n(\mathrm{Ag^+}) = 0.150\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 0.2500\text{ L} = 0.0375\text{ mol}$$ $$n(\mathrm{MgCl_2}) = 0.200\text{ mol L}^{-1} \times 0.1500\text{ L} = 0.0300\text{ mol}$$ $$n(\mathrm{Cl^-}) = 2 \times n(\mathrm{MgCl_2}) = 2 \times 0.0300\text{ mol} = 0.0600\text{ mol}$$
3. Identify Limiting Reagent:
Stoichiometry is $1:1$. We have $0.0375\text{ mol } \mathrm{Ag^+}$ and $0.0600\text{ mol } \mathrm{Cl^-}$.
Therefore, $\mathrm{Ag^+}$ is the **limiting reagent**.

4. Calculate mass of $\mathrm{AgCl_{(s)}}$ formed: $$n(\mathrm{AgCl}) = n(\mathrm{Ag^+}) = 0.0375\text{ mol}$$ $$M(\mathrm{AgCl}) = 107.87 + 35.45 = 143.32\text{ g mol}^{-1}$$ $$m(\mathrm{AgCl}) = 0.0375\text{ mol} \times 143.32\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{5.37\text{ g}} \quad (3\text{ sig. figs.})$$

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Calculates correct initial moles of $\mathrm{Ag^+}$ ($0.0375\text{ mol}$) and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ($0.0600\text{ mol}$).
  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies $\mathrm{Ag^+}$ as the limiting reagent.
  • 1 Mark: Deduces that $0.0375\text{ mol}$ of $\mathrm{AgCl}$ precipitate is formed.
  • 1 Mark: Calculates correct final mass $m = 5.37\text{ g}$ with correct units and significant figures.

Question 4 (Extension): Evaluating Qualitative Separation Protocols

5 Marks

A student is given a solution containing an unknown mixture of two cations: either ($\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$) OR ($\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$).

Construct an experimental flowchart testing protocol using standard laboratory reagents ($\mathrm{HCl_{(aq)}}$, $\mathrm{Na_2SO_{4(aq)}}$, $\mathrm{NaOH_{(aq)}}$) to unambiguously identify which pair of cations is present in the unknown mixture.

Hint: Compare chloride solubilities ($\mathrm{PbCl_2}$ is insoluble, $\mathrm{BaCl_2}$ and $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ are soluble) and sulfate/hydroxide solubilities.

Model Answer & Rubric

Model Answer:
• Step 1 (Test for $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$):
Add dilute $\mathrm{HCl_{(aq)}}$ to a portion of the unknown sample.
— Observation A: If a white precipitate forms ($\mathrm{PbCl_{2(s)}}$), $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ is PRESENT. The mixture MUST be ($\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$).
— Observation B: If NO precipitate forms, $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ is ABSENT. The mixture MUST be ($\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$).

• Step 2 (Differentiate $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ vs $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ if no chloride ppt):
If no precipitate formed in Step 1, take a fresh sample of the unknown and add sodium hydroxide solution ($\mathrm{NaOH_{(aq)}}$).
— $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ forms a white precipitate ($\mathrm{Ca(OH)_{2(s)}}$, slightly soluble / insoluble).
— $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ remains fully soluble ($\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}$ is soluble).
This confirms the presence of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ alongside $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$.

NESA Marking Schema:
  • 1 Mark: Proposes adding $\mathrm{HCl}$ first to selectively precipitate $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ as $\mathrm{PbCl_2}$.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly interprets white precipitate with $\mathrm{HCl}$ as confirming the ($\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}/\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$) pair.
  • 1 Mark: Explains that absence of precipitate with $\mathrm{HCl}$ eliminates $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ and indicates the ($\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}/\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$) pair.
  • 1 Mark: Proposes a valid secondary test ($\mathrm{NaOH}$ or flame test) to confirm $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$.
  • 1 Mark: Presents a clear, logically structured diagnostic pathway or flowchart.
